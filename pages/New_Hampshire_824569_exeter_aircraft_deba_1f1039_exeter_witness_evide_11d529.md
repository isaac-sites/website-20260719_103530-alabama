## Introduction

The [Exeter]({{ 'exeter/' | relative_url }}) incident became one of [New Hampshire]({{ 'why-new-hampshire-became-a-ufo-landmark/' | relative_url }})’s most famous UFO cases largely because it did not depend on a single witness. Within hours of the original report, two Exeter police officers independently described seeing lights that closely matched those reported by 18-year-old Norman Muscarello. That combination of civilian and law-enforcement testimony gave the case unusual weight and helped distinguish it from many other sightings of the 1960s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/New_Hampshire_824569_exeter_aircraft_deba_1f1039_exeter_witness_evide_11d529-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Witness Evidence illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/New_Hampshire_824569_exeter_aircraft_deba_1f1039_exeter_witness_evide_11d529-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/New_Hampshire_824569_exeter_aircraft_deba_1f1039_exeter_witness_evide_11d529-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Yet the strength of the witness evidence should not be confused with proof of what was seen. The Exeter witnesses established that several observers reported an unusual display of bright red lights under circumstances that appeared sincere and were documented promptly. What they did not establish was the precise nature, distance, size or origin of the source. The case remains important because it sits at the boundary between strong testimony and uncertain identification—a distinction that is central to the wider debate over military aircraft explanations.

## Muscarello's Account of the Five Sequenced Red Lights

Norman Muscarello’s testimony remains the foundation of the Exeter case. Walking along Route 150 near Kensington in the early hours of 3 September 1965, he reported seeing five bright red lights arranged in a line. According to contemporary descriptions, the lights flashed in sequence rather than simultaneously, creating a distinctive pattern that caught investigators’ attention. Muscarello initially thought he was seeing emergency vehicle lights, but became alarmed when the lights appeared elevated above the ground and illuminated nearby houses and fields.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

Several factors strengthen his credibility as a witness:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* He reported the sighting immediately rather than days or weeks later.
* He gave his account to police before the case became nationally known.
* He had no obvious public profile or financial incentive connected to the report.
* His description contained specific observational details, particularly the sequential flashing pattern of the lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

</div>

However, Muscarello’s testimony also contained important limitations. He primarily described lights rather than a clearly visible craft. He could not reliably determine distance, altitude or size. Like many night-time observers, he interpreted the display as being close to him, but without fixed reference points it is difficult to know whether the source was nearby, far away, low, or much higher than it appeared. These uncertainties matter because estimates of an object's size and behaviour depend heavily on distance. A distant aircraft can seem enormous if mistakenly judged to be close.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

One of the most discussed aspects of Muscarello’s account is the reported sequence of five red lights. Decades later, sceptical investigators Joe Nickell and retired Air Force pilot James McGaha argued that this pattern resembled the refuelling lights used on KC-97 tanker aircraft. Whether or not that explanation is correct, the fact that a specific lighting pattern was remembered and reported immediately gives Muscarello’s testimony more evidential value than a vague report of a distant light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

## What Officers Bertrand and Hunt Independently Confirmed

The strongest feature of the Exeter witness evidence is not Muscarello alone but the subsequent involvement of Officers Eugene Bertrand and David Hunt.

After hearing Muscarello’s account, Bertrand returned with him to the area to investigate. This was not a situation where officers merely recorded a statement and went home. Bertrand entered the location expecting either to verify or dismiss the report. Instead, he later stated that he saw a similar display of red lights himself. He was sufficiently disturbed by the sighting that he radioed for assistance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

Officer Hunt arrived shortly afterwards and also reported observing the lights. The three men then filed reports soon after the event. This sequence is significant because it reduces one of the common weaknesses in witness cases: the possibility that a dramatic story gradually develops through retelling. The core descriptions were recorded while memories were still fresh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

The officers independently reinforced several elements of Muscarello’s original account:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Bright red lights were present.
* The lights flashed in a distinctive sequence.
* The phenomenon appeared mobile rather than fixed.
* The display lasted long enough for repeated observation rather than a brief glance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

</div>

This convergence matters because independent corroboration is one of the strongest forms of witness evidence available when no photographs or instruments are present. The officers were not proving that the source was extraordinary, but they were strengthening the claim that something unusual was genuinely observed.

At the same time, the officers did not provide an independent measurement of distance or altitude. Their observations suffered from many of the same limitations as Muscarello’s. Seeing the same lights does not automatically resolve the question of what produced them.

## Why Credible Testimony Still Falls Short of Identification

The Exeter witnesses are often described as highly credible, and there are good reasons for that assessment. Muscarello reported the event promptly, while Bertrand and Hunt were trained observers in the sense that their police work required careful observation and reporting. Their accounts were broadly consistent on key points.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, credibility and identification are different questions.

A credible witness can accurately describe what they perceived while still being unable to determine what caused it. The Exeter observers established that bright red lights were seen. They did not establish whether those lights belonged to a military aircraft, a civilian aircraft, multiple aircraft, or something less easily explained.

Several factors limit the evidential reach of the testimony:

**Night-time viewing conditions.** Human perception of distance, speed and size deteriorates significantly in darkness, especially when only lights are visible.

**Lack of a clearly observed structure.** The most consistent descriptions concern lights. Reports of a large craft depend on interpretations of those lights rather than a sharply defined object.

**No instrumental confirmation.** There were no photographs, radar records directly tied to the sighting, or physical traces that could independently verify the witnesses’ impressions.

**Potential aviation activity.** The area lay near active military airspace associated with Pease Air Force Base, meaning aircraft-related explanations cannot be dismissed simply because the witnesses found them unconvincing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

These limitations do not weaken the witnesses' sincerity. Instead, they define what their testimony can legitimately prove.

## What the Witness Evidence Ultimately Establishes

The Exeter witness evidence is stronger than most UFO reports from the same era because it combines prompt reporting, multiple observers and police involvement. Few classic cases can point to three principal witnesses whose accounts were recorded so quickly after the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

What the testimony establishes with reasonable confidence is that several people observed an unusual pattern of bright red lights near Exeter and Kensington during the early morning hours of 3 September 1965. It also establishes that experienced local observers did not immediately recognise the display as a conventional aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>

What it does not establish is equally important. The witnesses could not reliably determine distance, size or identity. As a result, their testimony strengthens the case that something unusual was seen, but it does not by itself resolve the later debate over military aircraft. In the broader history of New Hampshire UFO reports, Exeter remains notable not because the witnesses proved an extraordinary craft was present, but because they produced some of the strongest and most carefully documented eyewitness evidence in the state's UFO record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exeter incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Exeter incident</span></span></span>
