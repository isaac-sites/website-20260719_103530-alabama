## Introduction

Neither incident provides convincing evidence of extraterrestrial visitation. The Mantell object was probably a high-altitude balloon, although surviving records do not identify a particular launch beyond doubt. The [Kelly-Hopkinsville]({{ 'kelly-encounter/' | relative_url }}) story rests largely on frightened witness testimony and has plausible explanations involving a meteor, owls and escalating confusion. Yet both cases matter because they show how military secrecy, incomplete evidence, press coverage and sincere human perception can combine to create enduring UFO legends. Later Kentucky reports continued the pattern: memorable testimony, occasional official involvement, but little durable physical or instrumented evidence.

## Why Kentucky became important so early

Kentucky entered modern UFO history at the moment the United States was beginning to treat unusual aerial reports as a potential defence problem. The Air Force programmes later grouped under Project Blue Book collected reports partly to determine whether unknown objects represented foreign aircraft, secret technology or threats to national security. The surviving Blue Book archive contains chronological case files, photographs and investigative material, although the programme ended in 1969 and did not examine later Kentucky sightings. Across the country, Blue Book received 12,618 reports and left 701 officially unidentified; the Air Force nevertheless concluded that none demonstrated extraterrestrial vehicles or technology beyond contemporary scientific knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Kentucky was especially well placed to become part of that story. Fort Knox, Godman Army Airfield, the Kentucky Air National Guard and nearby military installations meant that reports could quickly involve pilots, control towers and law-enforcement networks. That did not make the objects extraordinary, but it gave some sightings unusually serious witnesses and an official paper trail.

The state’s two landmark cases also arrived during periods of intense national interest. Mantell’s death came only months after the first great “flying saucer” wave of 1947. Kelly-Hopkinsville occurred in 1955, when newspaper readers were already familiar with spacecraft, Martians and Cold War fears. Those wider expectations shaped how ambiguous events were described and remembered, even when the witnesses themselves did not initially use the language later attached to their stories.

## The Mantell incident: a fatal pursuit near Fort Knox

On 7 January 1948, reports of a large unidentified object reached Godman Army Airfield at Fort Knox. Tower personnel could see something bright in the sky, and a flight of Kentucky Air National Guard F-51 Mustangs was asked to investigate. Captain Thomas F. Mantell continued climbing after other pilots turned back, reportedly because their aircraft lacked oxygen equipment for sustained high-altitude flight. Radio contact deteriorated, and Mantell’s aircraft later crashed, killing him. Investigators concluded that oxygen deprivation probably caused him to lose consciousness before the fighter entered an uncontrolled descent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dvidshub.net/news/436733/questions-remain-75-years-after-mysterious-fort-knox-ufo-incident-downed-pilot" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dvidshub.net">[DVIDS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dvidshub.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">News - Questions remain 75 years after mysterious Fort Knox UFO incident, downed pilot...</span></span></span>

The incident became one of the first UFO stories in which a military pilot had died while apparently pursuing an unknown object. Press reports rapidly introduced more dramatic possibilities, including claims that an alien craft had destroyed Mantell’s aircraft. The physical investigation did not support that interpretation. The aircraft’s condition and flight circumstances were consistent with a high-altitude climb, hypoxia and loss of control, not an attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dvidshub.net/news/436733/questions-remain-75-years-after-mysterious-fort-knox-ufo-incident-downed-pilot" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dvidshub.net">[DVIDS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dvidshub.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">News - Questions remain 75 years after mysterious Fort Knox UFO incident, downed pilot...</span></span></span>

### What was Mantell chasing?

The first official explanation suggested Venus, which was visible in the relevant part of the sky. That theory weakened because atmospheric conditions and the reported appearance and movement of the object did not fit the planet well. Attention subsequently shifted to a very large Skyhook research balloon, part of a then-secret high-altitude programme. Such balloons could look brilliant in sunlight, seem nearly stationary from the ground and reach altitudes far beyond an unpressurised fighter’s safe operating range.

A balloon remains the strongest explanation because witnesses south of Kentucky described a balloon-like object, and a classified high-altitude device would have been unfamiliar both to Mantell and to many personnel on the ground. The secrecy surrounding such programmes also helps explain why no simple answer was available at the time. However, no surviving document conclusively links a named Skyhook launch to the object seen over Kentucky that afternoon. Even the Fort Knox account prepared for the incident’s seventy-fifth anniversary describes the theory as likely but not finally proved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dvidshub.net/news/436733/questions-remain-75-years-after-mysterious-fort-knox-ufo-incident-downed-pilot" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dvidshub.net">[DVIDS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dvidshub.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">News - Questions remain 75 years after mysterious Fort Knox UFO incident, downed pilot...</span></span></span>

The most defensible judgement is therefore **probably explained**, rather than wholly unresolved or conclusively solved. Mantell almost certainly died from the hazards of a high-altitude pursuit. The precise identity of the distant object cannot be proved from the surviving record, but a research balloon fits the evidence far better than an extraterrestrial vehicle.

The case matters beyond the object itself. It demonstrated how a secret military programme could accidentally generate a UFO report and then prevent investigators from supplying a convincing public explanation. That gap between what observers saw and what officials could disclose became a recurring source of suspicion throughout the Cold War.

## Kelly-Hopkinsville: Kentucky’s most famous close encounter

On the night of 21 August 1955, members of an extended family at a farmhouse near Kelly, outside Hopkinsville, said that small figures repeatedly approached the house, appeared at windows and moved through the surrounding trees. Some witnesses described oversized glowing eyes, long arms, pointed ears or a helmet-like head. Men inside the house fired rifles and a shotgun at the figures before the group drove to Hopkinsville police headquarters for help.

Police officers, sheriff’s deputies, state troopers and military police from nearby Fort Campbell went to the property. They found a frightened household and evidence that weapons had been fired, including damaged screens and windows, but no bodies, unusual machinery or clear physical traces of intruders. Later retellings expanded the number and appearance of the supposed beings, helping to transform a confusing night-time episode into the story of the “Hopkinsville Goblins”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly%E2%80%93Hopkinsville_encounter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kelly–Hopkinsville encounter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kelly–Hopkinsville encounter</span></span></span>

The encounter is important because it involved several witnesses and a substantial police response rather than a lone observer reporting a distant light. Kentucky educational television has continued to preserve the family and local-history perspective, including testimony from a witness’s daughter and discussion of the event’s place in regional memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pbs.org/video/little-green-men-khvig8/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pbs.org">[PBS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pbs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">little green men khvig8</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kentucky Life &#124; Little Green Men &#124; Season 25 &#124; Episode 4February 23, 2023 — One of the UFO and alien encounters given the most credenc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2023</span></span></span> Yet the number of witnesses should not be confused with independent confirmation. Most were together in one tense environment, reacting to one another and viewing poorly illuminated targets through windows, doorways and trees.

### The strongest points in the family’s favour

The witnesses appeared genuinely frightened and sought police assistance, exposing themselves to scrutiny and ridicule rather than quietly promoting a commercial story. Contemporary accounts also reported that officers did not believe the group was simply drunk. Their broad descriptions of small figures with bright eyes remained recognisable across later versions, even though many details changed.

The police response establishes that a disturbance occurred. Guns were fired, the household believed something was outside, and multiple people maintained that belief. These facts make a simple invented hoax less persuasive than it might initially appear. They do not, however, establish what the witnesses saw.

No recovered object, biological trace, verified landing mark, photograph or instrument reading has emerged. Police did not find the alleged creatures, and the supposed spacecraft was based on one witness’s report of a bright object descending beyond the trees. The evidential core is therefore testimony produced under stress, darkness and repeated gunfire.

### Owls, a meteor and escalating fear

The leading sceptical interpretation combines several ordinary events rather than relying on one complete explanation. A meteor or other bright astronomical event could have supplied the initial impression of something descending beyond the farmhouse. Great horned owls, which are active at night, have large reflective eyes, prominent ear tufts and a defensive response near nests, could account for figures seen in trees or peering near the building. Their silent flight and ability to reappear from different positions would have been especially unsettling.

Once shooting began, ricochets, moving branches, shadows and startled animals could have reinforced the conviction that the property was under attack. Shared fear can improve agreement about the central claim while degrading reliable estimates of size, distance, speed and number. Psychologists have consequently used the Hopkinsville story as an example for teaching how extraordinary claims should be tested against alternative explanations and the limits of eyewitness evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00336/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Frontiers Hauntings, homeopathy, and the Hopkinsville Goblins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersHauntings, homeopathy, and the Hopkinsville GoblinsApril 17, 2014 — by R Schmaltz · 2014 · Cited by 63 — The purpose of this art...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 17, 2014</span></span></span>

The owl theory is plausible but not demonstrable. No owl was captured or photographed, and some reported movements do not map neatly onto ordinary bird behaviour. Calling the case completely debunked therefore goes too far. A fair classification is **unresolved at the level of exact reconstruction, but weak as evidence for anything extraordinary**. The surviving information supports misidentification and escalating alarm more strongly than visitors from another world.

The famous phrase “little green men” also illustrates how reporting altered the event. The witnesses’ creatures were generally described as greyish or silvery, not green. Later headlines and popular culture simplified a complicated set of descriptions into a memorable label, making the media version more durable than the original testimony.

## Flap reports and local clusters

Kentucky’s later UFO history contains many reports but few cases with evidence comparable even to Mantell’s [official records]({{ 'official-records-aced17/' | relative_url }}) or the police response at Kelly. One recurring form is the local “flap”: a period when several residents in one community report lights or objects, often after publicity encourages others to re-examine ambiguous experiences.

In 1978, WLKY reported that between roughly 25 and 50 people around Bloomfield had acknowledged seeing unexplained objects over a period of years. One resident described six lights above trees moving into formations. The surviving television archive is valuable as a record of local testimony and media interest, but it supplies no radar data, calibrated photographs or independent measurements with which to identify the lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wlky.com/article/ufos-aliens-kentucky-bloomfield-space-1978/39139597" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wlky.com">[WLKY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wlky.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufos aliens kentucky bloomfield space 1978</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs in Kentucky? In 1978, sightings were a regular occurrence in this small town...</span></span></span>

Such clusters are interesting because repeated reports can indicate a real recurring stimulus. That stimulus need not be exotic. Bright planets, aircraft on common routes, military training, meteors, balloons, atmospheric effects and later satellites can generate similar accounts among people viewing the same sky. Publicity can then increase reporting without any increase in unusual objects: people look upward more often, compare stories and become more willing to label a light a UFO.

The National UFO Reporting Center’s Kentucky index contains a large collection of public submissions from many decades, ranging from lights and discs to triangles and alleged close encounters. The database is useful for locating claims and identifying dates or geographical patterns, but it is not a catalogue of verified events. Entries are largely self-reported, contain uneven detail and may be submitted years after the supposed sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/subndx/?id=lKY" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUFORC Reports for State KYAugust 20, 2023 — NUFORC Reports for State KY. ## The 1993 Louisville police helicopter case

Kentucky’s most notable modern aviation-linked report occurred around midnight on 26 February 1993. Two Jefferson County police officers in a helicopter said that a small, glowing, pear-shaped object rose towards their altitude, moved rapidly around the aircraft and released three smaller fire-like objects. Two officers on the ground reportedly saw part of the episode. The helicopter crew contacted the airport, but no corresponding radar target or civilian reports were found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kentuckycopstories.wordpress.com/2020/04/19/close-encounter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kentuckycopstories.wordpress.com">[Kentucky Cop Stories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kentuckycopstories.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kentucky Cop Stories Close Encounter (Louisville, Ky) – Kentucky Cop Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kentucky Cop Stories Close Encounter (Louisville, Ky) – Kentucky Cop Stories</span></span></span>

The witnesses’ professional experience gives the case initial weight. Police helicopter crews are accustomed to judging aircraft movement and operating at night. There were also observers at more than one location. Even so, witness occupation does not remove the problems of distance, scale and relative motion. A small nearby object caught in rotor wash can appear to accelerate dramatically, while darkness removes the visual references needed for reliable speed estimates.

A local hobbyist subsequently said he had launched a miniature hot-air balloon made from lightweight plastic, balsa wood and candles in the area that night. In this explanation, the helicopter’s airflow produced the object’s erratic movement, while burning material or candles falling from the balloon created the apparent “fireballs”. Contemporary retrospective reporting describes the balloon as the principal sceptical solution, although the pilots and some UFO investigators rejected it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.leoweekly.com/news/little-green-men-in-the-bluegrass-15772118/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leoweekly.com">[leoweekly.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leoweekly.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Little green men in the bluegrass</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Little green men in the bluegrass</span></span></span>

Unlike many debunking suggestions, this one identifies a specific human activity in the correct area and period. It also explains the object’s small apparent size, glow and shedding of fiery material. The lack of a radar return is unsurprising for a lightweight homemade balloon. The harder part is reconciling the pilots’ impression of extreme controlled speed with an object moved by wind and rotor wash.

On balance, the Louisville incident is **plausibly explained by the homemade balloon**, though not reconstructed with enough technical data to eliminate every doubt. It is a useful warning against treating vivid manoeuvres as measured performance. No recorded range, triangulation or radar track existed from which actual velocity could be calculated.

## Military and aviation links without a hidden pattern

Kentucky’s best-known reports repeatedly involve military or aviation settings: Fort Knox and the Air National Guard in the Mantell case, military police from Fort Campbell at Kelly, and a police aircraft in Louisville. This can make the state appear to possess a special connection between UFOs and military activity.

The simpler interpretation is that aviation institutions create better reporting opportunities. Pilots, air-traffic personnel, police and military units have radios, logs and established chains for responding to possible hazards. Their involvement makes incidents more visible and better documented than equally puzzling observations by isolated civilians. It does not, by itself, show that unexplained objects were targeting bases.

Military secrecy nevertheless played a genuine role in historical confusion. Classified balloons and reconnaissance systems could be mistaken for unfamiliar aircraft, while investigators might be unable to disclose the relevant programme. Modern government reviews acknowledge the same basic problem: an image of an ordinary object may remain classified because the sensor, location or collection method is sensitive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Dr_Jon_Kosloski_Statement_for_the_Record_SASC_Open_Hearing_Nov2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span> This creates a space in which mundane explanations can look evasive and unresolved cases can acquire conspiracy narratives.

There is no strong public evidence that Kentucky experienced a sustained radar-confirmed intrusion, repeated interference with military systems or a documented pattern of extraordinary craft around its installations. The state’s military connection is historically important, but it should not be overstated.

## What the evidence actually supports

Kentucky’s UFO record becomes clearer when cases are separated by evidential strength rather than fame.

**Probably explained:** The Mantell object was most likely a high-altitude balloon, while Mantell’s death is well accounted for by hypoxia and loss of control. The 1993 Louisville helicopter object has a credible, locally identified homemade-balloon explanation.

**Unresolved but weak:** Kelly-Hopkinsville cannot be reconstructed with certainty, but its evidence is almost entirely eyewitness testimony. Owls, a meteor and collective alarm offer a coherent conventional account without proving exactly what each witness saw.

**Reported clusters without verification:** Bloomfield and numerous later public reports establish continuing interest and repeated observations, not the existence of anomalous vehicles. Most lack the time-stamped imagery, radar, range information or independent sensor records needed for rigorous analysis.

This distinction matters because “unidentified” is a description of the available information, not a claim about origin. A case can remain unidentified because the evidence is incomplete, contradictory or collected too late. The United States government’s current All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office similarly reports that many cases resolve into birds, balloons or unmanned systems, while others remain open because too little data exists. It has stated that it has found no verifiable evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity or technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Dr_Jon_Kosloski_Statement_for_the_Record_SASC_Open_Hearing_Nov2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

Modern scientific proposals for studying unusual aerial phenomena emphasise multiple synchronised sensors: wide- and narrow-field cameras, triangulation, radar-derived range and motion, spectral measurements, environmental monitoring and reliable timing. Those are precisely the elements missing from Kentucky’s classic stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the Kentucky stories endure

The state’s UFO history endures because its leading cases contain powerful human and institutional elements. Mantell was a real pilot who died during an official pursuit. The Kelly household genuinely summoned armed police after a frightening night. Louisville’s helicopter crew were experienced officers who believed they had encountered something capable of extraordinary movement. These are not merely anonymous internet anecdotes.

At the same time, later investigation has generally weakened the extraordinary interpretations. Secret-balloon history made the Mantell case less mysterious. Natural and psychological explanations exposed the weaknesses of Kelly-Hopkinsville. A named balloon builder supplied a credible source for the Louisville object. No later release has produced alien technology, unambiguous photographs or supporting radar data from these incidents.

Kentucky therefore offers a particularly useful history of UFO ambiguity. Its cases show that sincere witnesses can encounter something genuinely unidentified without the cause being extraordinary; that official involvement does not guarantee strong evidence; and that later reporting may preserve drama more faithfully than detail. The state’s UFO stories remain culturally significant and, in places, historically unresolved, but the public record supports misidentification, secrecy and incomplete observation far more strongly than extraterrestrial visitation.

  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {

  function availableMarkets(section) {

        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });

    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;

    });

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });

      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;

    }

      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
