## Introduction

The short answer is no: the available record does not show that two separate radar systems continuously tracked the same object during the January 1952 Nenana incident. What makes the case unusual is that both a ground radar station at [Murphy Dome]({{ 'murphy-dome/' | relative_url }}) and the airborne radar aboard an F-94 interceptor reported unexplained returns during the same mission. However, the timing and circumstances of those detections do not demonstrate that they were observing one common target. ## What Murphy Dome and the F-94 Each Detected

Murphy Dome, a Ground-Control Intercept station near Fairbanks, was part of [Alaska]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-alaskas-skies/' | relative_url }})'s early Cold War air-defence network. Its role was to detect unknown aircraft and guide interceptors toward them. On 22 January 1952, operators reported an unusual radar return that appeared to move at extraordinary speed, reverse direction and then disappear. After adjustments to the radar settings, the return was lost and later reappeared before fading again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Project_Blue_Book%2C_BBA-PBSR6-300.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book, BBA PBSR6 300</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsThe Project Blue Book ArchiveProject Blue Book was the code name of the U.S. Air Force&#x27;s UFO investigation.... The F-94 Starfire involved in the incident carried its own airborne interception radar. According to Project Blue Book summaries, the aircraft later obtained radar contacts near Nenana. During one encounter the radar operator reported two returns, described as one bright and one faint. During another, the crew again acquired a target and appeared to close to a very short indicated range before the contact vanished. Neither event produced a visual sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Project_Blue_Book%2C_BBA-PBSR6-300.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book, BBA PBSR6 300</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsThe Project Blue Book ArchiveProject Blue Book was the code name of the U.S. Air Force&#x27;s UFO investigation.... ## Why the Contacts Were Not Simultaneous

The key weakness in the “two radars tracked one object” interpretation is the sequence of events.

The Murphy Dome operators initially detected an unidentified return and directed attention toward it. Yet the station subsequently lost that contact. Project Blue Book's account states that the ground radar no longer had the unknown target under observation while it continued to track the interceptor itself. The airborne radar contacts occurred later as the F-94 approached the Nenana area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Project_Blue_Book%2C_BBA-PBSR6-300.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book, BBA PBSR6 300</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsThe Project Blue Book ArchiveProject Blue Book was the code name of the U.S. Air Force&#x27;s UFO investigation.... In other words:

* The ground radar did not maintain an uninterrupted track.
* The airborne radar detections occurred after the original ground contact had disappeared.
* Published summaries do not describe both radars observing the same target at the same time.
* No surviving record shows a coordinated radar plot proving that the airborne contacts occupied the same position as the earlier Murphy Dome return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Project_Blue_Book%2C_BBA-PBSR6-300.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book, BBA PBSR6 300</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsThe Project Blue Book ArchiveProject Blue Book was the code name of the U.S. Air Force&#x27;s UFO investigation.... This timing gap is crucial. Radar operators can only demonstrate that two systems tracked the same object when the tracks overlap in time and location or can be reliably handed off from one system to another. The Nenana case lacks that kind of documented handoff. Instead, the evidence consists of one unexplained ground-radar episode followed by separate airborne-radar detections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Project_Blue_Book%2C_BBA-PBSR6-300.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book, BBA PBSR6 300</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsThe Project Blue Book ArchiveProject Blue Book was the code name of the U.S. Air Force&#x27;s UFO investigation.... ## What Independent Radar Returns Can and Cannot Prove

The existence of multiple radar reports is still significant. It makes the case harder to dismiss than a lone visual sighting because trained operators reported instrument-based detections under operational conditions. The F-94's radar was a separate system from the ground installation, reducing the likelihood that exactly the same equipment fault produced every reported contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Project_Blue_Book%2C_BBA-PBSR6-300.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: upload.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">upload.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book, BBA PBSR6 300</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsThe Project Blue Book ArchiveProject Blue Book was the code name of the U.S. Air Force&#x27;s UFO investigation.... At 1020Z...</span></span></span>

However, independent radar returns have limits.

They can suggest that unusual conditions existed in the area, but they do not by themselves identify a target. Early-1950s radar systems could be affected by atmospheric conditions, propagation anomalies, temperature inversions and interpretation errors. ## Why This Detail Matters to the Nenana Case

The distinction between “two radars detected something” and “two radars tracked the same object” changes how the incident should be assessed.

If Murphy Dome and the F-94 had maintained simultaneous contact with a single target, the case would provide much stronger evidence that a real object moved through Alaskan airspace.
