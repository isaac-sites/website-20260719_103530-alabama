## Introduction

[Wyoming]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-wyomings-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s 1970s abduction stories are important less because they prove that anyone was taken by extraterrestrials than because they show how an unusual experience can grow into a much fuller narrative. A puzzling light, a period of unaccounted time or a confused return from a remote hunting trip could later acquire occupants, spacecraft interiors, medical examinations or messages for humanity—especially after [hypnosis]({{ 'hypnosis-7d67f4/' | relative_url }}), repeated interviews and years of public retelling.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Wyoming_ed190c_abduction_memory_arc_2e68ce-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Memory Claims" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The state’s clearest examples are the claims associated with hunter Carl Higdon and rancher Pat McGuire. Both men may have undergone experiences they found genuinely disturbing, but the most elaborate parts of their stories were not independently recorded as they happened. They emerged through later recollection, interpretation and, in McGuire’s case, extensive hypnotic sessions. Modern memory research gives good reason to treat such detail cautiously: hypnosis can increase the amount a person reports and their confidence in it without reliably showing that the added material is accurate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cowboystatedaily.com/2023/09/30/alien-abductions-of-2-wyoming-men-in-the-1970s-remain-unexplainable/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cowboystatedaily.com">[cowboystatedaily.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cowboystatedaily.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">alien abductions of 2 wyoming men in the 1970s remain unexplainable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cowboy State DailyAlien Abductions Of 2 Wyoming Men In The 1970s Remain Unexplainable &#124; Cowboy State Daily...</span></span></span>

## From distant lights to missing-time stories

Earlier Wyoming UFO records generally concern objects in the sky: lights, discs, fast-moving shapes or unfamiliar aircraft-like phenomena. The abduction narratives of the 1970s shifted the emphasis from an external observation to an internal personal history. The central question was no longer merely “What was in the sky?” but “What happened to me during the time I cannot explain?”

That change can be seen in Pat McGuire’s story. According to a later account based partly on family recollections, McGuire and a relative became lost during an elk-hunting trip in the Tetons in October 1973. They noticed an orange glow and eventually returned to their vehicle with several hours apparently unaccounted for. At that stage, the incident could be described relatively simply: disorientation in difficult country, an unusual light and uncertainty about elapsed time. The much more detailed claim that McGuire had encountered beings developed later, alongside reports of repeated UFO visits to his ranch near Bosler, cattle deaths and supposed communications from what he called “Star People”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cowboystatedaily.com/2023/09/30/alien-abductions-of-2-wyoming-men-in-the-1970s-remain-unexplainable/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cowboystatedaily.com">[Cowboy State Daily]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cowboystatedaily.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">alien abductions of 2 wyoming men in the 1970s remain unexplainable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cowboy State DailyAlien Abductions Of 2 Wyoming Men In The 1970s Remain Unexplainable &#124; Cowboy State Daily...</span></span></span>

McGuire filed a report with the Aerial Phenomena Research Organization in 1976, claiming that unusual craft had repeatedly visited his ranch. Later stories included landed objects, figures visible through windows, a witness approaching a craft in a trance and failed attempts to photograph the events. The absence of successful photographs or other independently testable records means that the later episodes cannot be checked in the way an aircraft track, radar return or time-stamped image might be checked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cowboystatedaily.com/2023/09/30/alien-abductions-of-2-wyoming-men-in-the-1970s-remain-unexplainable/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cowboystatedaily.com">[Cowboy State Daily]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cowboystatedaily.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">alien abductions of 2 wyoming men in the 1970s remain unexplainable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cowboy State DailyAlien Abductions Of 2 Wyoming Men In The 1970s Remain Unexplainable &#124; Cowboy State Daily...</span></span></span>

Carl Higdon’s case followed a similar movement from a strange and poorly documented event towards a highly structured abduction account. On 25 October 1974, Higdon disappeared for several hours while elk hunting in the Medicine Bow National Forest area south of Rawlins. He was later found confused and physically unwell. His eventual account described a humanoid being, an unusual projectile, transport inside a transparent or enclosed craft and a journey to another location. The University of Wyoming archive catalogue confirms that R. Leo Sprinkle’s surviving files include a typescript of a Carl Higdon interview dated 1974, making the case unusually significant within the state’s archival record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archiveswest.orbiscascade.org/ark%3A80444/xv805708" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archiveswest.orbiscascade.org">[Archives West]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archiveswest.orbiscascade.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives West R. Leo Sprinkle papers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives WestR. Leo Sprinkle papers - Archives West...</span></span></span>

The difficulty is separating what Higdon said immediately from details stabilised through subsequent questioning, publication and discussion. Contemporary physical circumstances—his disappearance, his condition when found and whatever search activity occurred—deserve to be distinguished from claims about an alien named “Ausso”, transportation between worlds or other extraordinary details. The former may be matters of record; the latter depend mainly on testimony.

This does not mean that Higdon or McGuire must have consciously invented their experiences. Missing-time reports can arise from ordinary failures of time estimation, fatigue, shock, disorientation, sleep-related experiences or gaps in memory. Once a person believes that something extraordinary occurred during the gap, later images, dreams and suggestions can be interpreted as fragments of the missing event. Research on people reporting alien abduction memories has found greater susceptibility to certain forms of false recognition, supporting a psychological mechanism without showing that every individual account has the same cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12150421/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">false recognition in 3 groups: people reporting recovered memories of alien abduction. people who believe they were abducted by aliens bu...</span></span></span>

## R. Leo Sprinkle and the Wyoming archives

R. Leo Sprinkle gave Wyoming an unusual place in the history of abduction research. A counselling psychologist at the University of Wyoming, he had professional interests in hypnosis, the psychology of UFO experiences and parapsychology. He investigated contact and abduction claims, corresponded with witnesses and UFO researchers, appeared in television programmes and later founded the Rocky Mountain Conference on UFO Investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archiveswest.orbiscascade.org/ark%3A80444/xv805708" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archiveswest.orbiscascade.org">[Archives West]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archiveswest.orbiscascade.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives West R. Leo Sprinkle papers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives WestR. Leo Sprinkle papers - Archives West...</span></span></span>

His papers are now held by the American Heritage Center at the University of Wyoming. The collection covers material dating from 1961 to 2020 and contains correspondence, questionnaires, local and regional reports, interview transcripts, manuscripts, conference papers, newsletters and original research. Its catalogue lists folders for local UFO reports, Wyoming regional reports, UFO questionnaires and the 1974 Higdon interview. These records make the archive valuable for studying how reports were collected and how investigators, witnesses and contactee networks influenced one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archiveswest.orbiscascade.org/ark%3A80444/xv805708" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archiveswest.orbiscascade.org">[Archives West]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archiveswest.orbiscascade.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives West R. Leo Sprinkle papers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives WestR. Leo Sprinkle papers - Archives West...</span></span></span>

The archive’s existence should not, however, be mistaken for official confirmation of the events described within it. Archives preserve claims, correspondence and research practices; they do not automatically validate their contents. A witness transcript establishes that a statement was made. It does not establish that the recalled event occurred exactly as stated.

The structure of the Sprinkle papers is itself historically revealing. Material concerning conventional psychology sits alongside UFO contactees, paranormal experiences, reincarnation, near-death accounts and later spiritual interpretations. That mixture illustrates how the meaning of “abduction” broadened over time. What began as an investigation of anomalous observations increasingly overlapped with ideas about psychic development, cosmic purpose and personal transformation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archiveswest.orbiscascade.org/ark%3A80444/xv805708" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archiveswest.orbiscascade.org">[Archives West]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archiveswest.orbiscascade.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archives West R. Leo Sprinkle papers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archives WestR. Leo Sprinkle papers - Archives West...</span></span></span>

Sprinkle’s own position also appears to have evolved. Wyoming Public Media’s summary of his early work notes that he examined whether UFO reports could have natural, social or psychological origins and complained that the press often misrepresented his ideas. His later reputation was more closely associated with accepting contactee experiences and using hypnosis to explore them. That change matters because an interviewer’s expectations can shape the direction of an investigation even without deliberate coaching.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wyomingpublicmedia.org/show/archives-on-the-air/2018-10-24/archives-on-the-air-73-ufo-psychology-leo-sprinkle-papers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wyomingpublicmedia.org">[wyomingpublicmedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wyomingpublicmedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wyomingpublicmedia.org.</span></span></span>

For researchers, the strongest use of the archive is therefore comparative. An early statement can be placed beside a later transcript, published article, conference presentation or family recollection. Additions can then be dated rather than treated as if they were all present from the beginning. The archive may reveal consistency, but it may also show narrative expansion: new beings, messages, journeys or explanations appearing only after repeated discussion.

Access restrictions further affect what can presently be concluded. The finding aid states that users must sign an agreement limiting the recording or reproduction of personal names, while several boxes are closed until 2095. ## How hypnosis and retelling affect testimony

Hypnosis was attractive to 1970s UFO investigators because it appeared to offer a way of recovering information hidden behind amnesia or “missing time”. In practice, hypnotic recall is not equivalent to playing back a recording. Memory is reconstructive: people rebuild an event from retained impressions, expectations, later information and the context in which they are asked to remember.

Laboratory and clinical research has repeatedly warned that hypnosis can encourage more reporting without guaranteeing more truth. It may also increase confidence in inaccurate recollections. A person can therefore leave a session with a detailed, emotionally powerful and sincerely believed account that is less reliable than its vividness suggests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11832514/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PMCthe role of hypnosis in memory recall and false</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by DG Leo · 2025 · Cited by 11 — Hypnosis induces an increase in parasympathetic activity and a decrease in sympathetic activity, sugg...</span></span></span>

Several mechanisms are especially relevant to Wyoming’s abduction narratives:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Suggestion through questions.** Asking what a witness saw “inside the craft” assumes that there was a craft and an interior. Even subtle wording can supply a framework for uncertain images.
* **Source confusion.** A person may remember a scene but lose track of whether it came from the original experience, a dream, a newspaper story, an investigator’s theory or a television dramatisation.
* **Imagination becoming familiarity.** Repeatedly visualising a possibility can make it feel remembered. Familiarity is then mistaken for evidence of prior experience.
* **Confidence inflation.** Retelling tends to smooth uncertainty. “I think I saw” may gradually become “I saw”, particularly when listeners reward detail and consistency.
* **Narrative selection.** Contradictory or vague fragments may disappear from published accounts, while striking details are repeated until they become the recognised version of the story.

</div>

The evolution of McGuire’s account is a particularly sharp warning. Later reporting says that Sprinkle conducted numerous hypnosis sessions with him. The recovered narrative grew to include human-like Star People, predictions of climatic disaster, instructions about the location of water, religious symbolism and claims of political or historical significance. Friends and relatives reportedly believed that McGuire became increasingly paranoid and distressed as the story expanded. These recollections are secondary and cannot establish precisely what hypnosis caused, but they do show that a larger narrative did not produce stronger independent evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cowboystatedaily.com/2023/09/30/alien-abductions-of-2-wyoming-men-in-the-1970s-remain-unexplainable/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cowboystatedaily.com">[Cowboy State Daily]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cowboystatedaily.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">alien abductions of 2 wyoming men in the 1970s remain unexplainable</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cowboy State DailyAlien Abductions Of 2 Wyoming Men In The 1970s Remain Unexplainable &#124; Cowboy State Daily...</span></span></span>

Cultural expectations matter because witnesses do not interpret blank periods in isolation. By the mid-1970s, American audiences already knew the widely publicised Betty and Barney Hill case, while stories involving landed craft, humanoid occupants and abduction were appearing in books, television and UFO publications. ## What can responsibly be concluded?

Wyoming’s 1970s abduction cases are not worthless simply because their extraordinary claims remain unverified. They preserve evidence of real human experiences: fear, confusion, lost time, social ridicule and the search for an explanation. Higdon’s disappearance and distressed condition deserve historical attention, as do the reports, interviews and correspondence preserved in Wyoming archives.

Yet testimony must be weighted according to when and how it emerged. An immediate, independently recorded observation is generally more useful than a detail first reported years later. A detail recovered during hypnosis is weaker still unless it can be corroborated by evidence that existed before the session. Agreement between a hypnotised witness and an investigator’s expectations is not independent confirmation.

A sensible assessment separates the surviving material into three levels:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Contemporaneous circumstances:** a person was missing, disoriented, distressed or reported an unusual light.
2. **Early testimony:** what the witness said before extensive interviewing, publicity or hypnosis.
3. **Retrospective narrative:** later occupants, journeys, messages and interpretations added through recall and retelling.

</div>

For Wyoming’s best-known claims, the first two levels may contain genuine unresolved questions. The third is the most memorable but also the least secure. No publicly documented physical evidence independently confirms that Higdon, McGuire or another Wyoming witness was transported aboard a non-human craft.

The larger historical lesson is that narrative strength and evidential strength are different things. Hypnosis, archives and repeated publication can make a story richer, more consistent and more culturally durable. They cannot, by themselves, make it more factually reliable. Wyoming’s abduction memories therefore belong in the state’s UFO history primarily as evidence of how extraordinary claims were formed, investigated and preserved—not as proof that the events remembered under hypnosis occurred.
