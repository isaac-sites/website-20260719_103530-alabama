## Introduction

[Hawaii]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-hawaiis-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s Cold War UFO files show a real official reporting system, but not a hidden collection of proven extraordinary craft. Military personnel, pilots and civilians sent unusual aerial observations through Air Force channels because the islands were strategically important and surrounded by active naval and aviation traffic. Investigators then classified most reports as balloons, aircraft, astronomical objects or insufficiently observed lights. In the Air Force summary supplied to a Honolulu newspaper in 1952, only one of 11 Hawaiian reports remained unexplained: a binocular sighting by submarine personnel off Lahaina, Maui.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/1952e.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - UFO REPORTS 1952...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. An official file confirms that a report was received, recorded and sometimes investigated. It does not confirm that the object behaved exactly as estimated, that every ordinary explanation was eliminated or that the Air Force endorsed an exotic interpretation. Hawaii’s records are historically valuable chiefly because they reveal how uncertainty was processed — and how often the surviving evidence was too slight to support a firm conclusion.

## Project Blue Book reports from Hawaii

Project Blue Book was the US Air Force’s principal public UFO investigation from 1952 to 1969, following the earlier Project Sign and Project Grudge inquiries. Its purpose was to assess whether unidentified objects posed a national-security threat and to analyse reported sightings. Across the entire programme, the Air Force recorded 12,618 reports and left 701 classified as unidentified, while stating that it had found no evidence of extraterrestrial vehicles or technology beyond contemporary scientific knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104590/unidentified-flying-objects-and-air-force-project-blue-book/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[U.S. Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unidentified Flying Objects and Air Force Project Blue Book &gt; Air Force &gt; Fact Sheet Display...</span></span></span>

The National Archives describes a typical Blue Book case file as a mixture of witness questionnaires, letters or telegrams, correspondence, press cuttings and occasional analysis of photographs or physical material. A control sheet usually summarised the report and recorded the Air Force’s evaluation. This structure gave even modest sightings the appearance of a formal investigation, although the amount and quality of supporting evidence varied sharply from case to case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/news/articles/project-blue-book-50th-anniversary" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

Hawaii’s early pattern can be reconstructed from an Air Force compilation published by the *Honolulu Star-Bulletin* on 12 November 1952. It listed 11 reports dating from July 1947 to August 1952:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* three reports at Hickam Air Force Base were judged probably to involve balloons;
* three flame-like or tailed objects were classified as astronomical;
* a silvery disc reported at Lihue, Kauai, was considered probably a balloon;
* a white object seen from Honolulu was labelled a balloon;
* two cream-coloured, plate-like objects near Hickam were considered possible aircraft;
* another shiny metallic object was judged probably to be a balloon;
* the Lahaina submarine report remained unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/1952e.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - UFO REPORTS 1952...</span></span></span>

This is a more revealing picture than a simple count of “UFO cases”. The reports entered official channels because their identity was initially uncertain, but the dominant official interpretation was conventional. Balloons were especially relevant in an era when military and meteorological balloon programmes were common, while bright planets, meteors and aircraft lights could be difficult to identify without accurate information about direction, duration, angular movement and weather.

The compilation also stated that no photographs of the Hawaiian objects were available. That absence limited investigators to witness descriptions, often written after the event and without reliable measurements of distance, altitude or speed. A light that appears to cross the sky rapidly may actually be nearby and slow, or distant and nearly stationary; without range data, apparently dramatic performance cannot be calculated securely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/1952e.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - UFO REPORTS 1952...</span></span></span>

## The Lahaina case that remained unknown

The most significant early entry occurred on 16 July 1952, when personnel aboard the submarine USS *Bugara* reported an object off Lahaina. According to the Air Force summary, the witnesses viewed it through binoculars and described an oval disc whose colour varied from white to red as it moved across the sky. The report was the only one among the 11 Hawaiian cases that Air Force evaluators had not explained by November 1952.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/1952e.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - UFO REPORTS 1952...</span></span></span>

The military setting gives the case greater historical interest than an isolated anonymous sighting. Submarine personnel were accustomed to observing aircraft, ships, navigation lights and atmospheric conditions, and binocular observation is more useful than a brief unaided glimpse. The case also arose during the major American UFO wave of summer 1952, when a sharp rise in reports placed heavy pressure on the Air Force’s small investigative system.

Yet the surviving public summary preserves too little information for a strong modern assessment. It gives no precise observing time, angular path, duration, bearing, weather record or independent radar confirmation. It does not establish whether all witnesses produced separate statements, whether the object’s changing colour resulted from atmospheric effects, or whether aircraft and astronomical objects were systematically checked. “Unknown” therefore means that the Air Force did not reach a satisfactory identification from the information available; it does not mean that investigators demonstrated extraordinary technology.

This limitation runs through Blue Book as a whole. A report could remain unidentified because it contained impressive, conflicting data, but it could also remain unidentified because it arrived late, lacked basic measurements or could not be checked against complete operational records. The *Bugara* sighting deserves a place in Hawaii’s UFO history, but its evidential strength is lower than its official classification may suggest.

## The 1956 Maui investigation

A later Maui file shows Blue Book’s investigative reasoning in much. At about 11.15 pm on 20 January 1956, a 56-year-old retired woman in Lahaina reported an orange, flame-coloured object accompanied by what she described as a muffled roar. She said it approached rapidly, slowed or hovered and appeared to move towards a valley on Molokai before fading from view. The observation reportedly lasted about 25 minutes and was made through a screened window.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mauinews.com/news/local-news/2015/02/declassified-documents-include-maui-ufo-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mauinews.com">[mauinews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mauinews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Declassified documents include Maui UFO report &#124; News, Sports, Jobs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maui News...</span></span></span>

Lieutenant Colonel Harold Bullock interviewed the witness and described her as mature, sincere, intelligent and respectable. That assessment is important because it shows that the Air Force did not simply dismiss her as dishonest or irrational. She had contacted the Civil Aeronautics Authority after failing to identify the light and reportedly said that, despite having read a flying-saucer book, she did not accept its claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mauinews.com/news/local-news/2015/02/declassified-documents-include-maui-ufo-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mauinews.com">[mauinews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mauinews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Declassified documents include Maui UFO report &#124; News, Sports, Jobs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maui News...</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, Bullock concluded that the object was probably an aircraft. He argued that aircraft exhaust or navigation lights, altered by diffusion and refraction in dense air near the horizon, could account for the reported colour and apparent size. He also tested the witness’s sense of angular scale: she was surprised that a small coin held at arm’s length could cover a substantial part of the Moon, weakening her estimate that the object had appeared unusually large.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mauinews.com/news/local-news/2015/02/declassified-documents-include-maui-ufo-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mauinews.com">[mauinews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mauinews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Declassified documents include Maui UFO report &#124; News, Sports, Jobs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maui News...</span></span></span>

The case demonstrates both the value and the weakness of Blue Book’s method. Investigators considered witness character, viewing conditions, aircraft activity and errors in estimating size. They did not, however, identify a particular aeroplane. The Civil Aeronautics Authority had no flight recorded at the reported time, while Bullock noted that local military and civilian traffic was extensive and that complete navigation logs were not kept for local flights. Checking every possible pilot would have been impractical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mauinews.com/news/local-news/2015/02/declassified-documents-include-maui-ufo-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mauinews.com">[mauinews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mauinews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Declassified documents include Maui UFO report &#124; News, Sports, Jobs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maui News...</span></span></span>

The final “aircraft” classification was therefore an informed judgement rather than a demonstrated match. It was plausible because the report included an apparent engine-like sound, aircraft-style movement and a low-horizon view through atmospheric haze. It was not conclusive because no corresponding flight was found. The distinction is useful: the case is better described as plausibly explained than definitively solved.

The Lahaina report also illustrates why the number of searchable “Hawaii documents” should not be mistaken for the number of distinct, strong cases. Searches of the digitised collection have returned material associated with Honolulu, Hilo, Kauai, Hickam, Pearl Harbor, Barbers Point, Haleiwa, Ewa Beach and Kunia, but individual sightings may generate several pages or documents. ## The Dan Kimball story and its archival problems

One of the most frequently repeated Hawaii-linked Cold War stories involves Dan A. Kimball, Secretary of the Navy from 1951 to 1953. Later UFO literature claimed that two disc-like objects approached or passed a Navy aircraft carrying Kimball during a Pacific journey in 1952. The story became influential because it involved a Cabinet-level defence official and was said to have encouraged Navy interest in UFO reports.

The problem is not necessarily that nothing happened, but that the best-known narrative depends heavily on later retellings. A pro-UFO archival page says that a contemporary article was eventually located among Blue Book materials, yet it also acknowledges disagreement over the date. The popular version usually describes Kimball as flying “to Hawaii”, while later timeline reconstruction has argued that the precise route and stage of his Pacific journey may have been misreported.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/520314hawaii_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportNavy Secretary Dan Kimball was flying to Hawaii when two disc-shaped craft streaked in toward his Navy executive plane. ac...</span></span></span>

This makes the Kimball incident different from the Lahaina reports. The 1956 case has a named Air Force investigator, recorded interview, explicit reasoning and formal conclusion. The Kimball account survives publicly mainly as a historical claim repeated in UFO publications and supported by press material preserved in an official collection. A newspaper clipping inside a government file is evidence that the story circulated and was retained; it is not automatically an Air Force-verified incident report.

The witness’s seniority also cannot substitute for missing technical evidence. A Navy Secretary could honestly observe something unfamiliar, but determining what it was would still require independent crew statements, exact flight information, weather data, aircraft positions and, ideally, radar or photographic records. Without those elements, Kimball’s status increases the story’s historical importance more than its physical evidential strength.

The safest assessment is that the episode may reflect a genuine report involving Kimball’s aircraft, but the familiar “two discs near Hawaii” version is not securely established by a complete, accessible official investigation. It belongs in Hawaii’s Cold War UFO history as an influential and disputed archival story, not as one of the state’s best-documented cases.

## Why Hawaii’s files are strategically significant

During the Cold War, an unidentified object near Hawaii did not have to be extraterrestrial to matter. The islands contained major airfields, naval facilities and Pacific command infrastructure. Unknown aircraft, reconnaissance systems, balloons or missiles could all have defence significance. Recording unusual reports was therefore a rational intelligence function even when investigators expected ordinary explanations.

Blue Book’s remit reflected that priority. The Air Force examined UFO reports partly to determine whether any represented a threat to national security, not simply to answer public questions about visitors from space. Its final conclusions were that no investigated report had demonstrated such a threat, no unidentified case had shown scientific principles beyond modern knowledge, and no evidence established extraterrestrial vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104590/unidentified-flying-objects-and-air-force-project-blue-book/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[U.S. Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unidentified Flying Objects and Air Force Project Blue Book &gt; Air Force &gt; Fact Sheet Display...</span></span></span>

Hawaii also presented unusual practical difficulties. Sightings over the ocean often lacked landmarks against which to estimate distance or speed. Records of short local flights were incomplete, while military operations could be sensitive or poorly accessible to Blue Book staff. Investigators working from Ohio might receive forms after the event rather than conduct immediate fieldwork. In those circumstances, both false certainty and unresolved ambiguity were predictable outcomes.

The files therefore preserve two overlapping histories. One is the history of what people saw: discs, flames, metallic objects and coloured lights. The other is the history of how a Cold War bureaucracy converted those observations into categories such as balloon, aircraft, astronomical or unknown. The second history is often better documented than the first.

## Why an official file is not proof

The National Archives makes an important distinction about records in its custody: certifying a copy confirms that it reproduces an archived document, not that the statements within the document are true. That principle applies directly to UFO files. Government custody establishes provenance, but not the accuracy of every witness estimate, press report or investigator’s conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Three levels of claim should therefore be kept separate:

**The report existed.** This is usually the strongest conclusion. A dated form, letter or control sheet can establish that somebody reported an object and that an agency recorded it.

**The witness accurately described an unusual observation.** This may be probable where the account is contemporary and internally consistent, but estimates of size, speed and distance remain vulnerable to perception errors.

**The object was genuinely extraordinary.** This requires evidence beyond an unexplained classification: reliable position and timing data, independent observations, radar or instrument records, imagery with a clear chain of custody, or physical evidence. The Hawaiian Cold War files rarely preserve that level of corroboration.

Official explanations also need scrutiny. Labels such as “aircraft” or “balloon” were sometimes supported by identifiable matches, but in other cases they represented the investigator’s best fit. Conversely, an “unknown” classification did not certify an exotic craft. It could simply mark the point at which the available information ran out.

## What the files really establish

Hawaii’s official Cold War UFO record is neither empty nor sensational. It shows that unexplained aerial reports were taken seriously enough to be collected, especially when they involved military locations, naval personnel or strategically important airspace. It also shows that most early Hawaiian cases were assigned ordinary explanations and that even the better-documented investigations often lacked complete flight, radar or photographic evidence.

The 1952 USS *Bugara* report remains the clearest early Hawaiian “unknown”, but its surviving public summary is too thin to support confident conclusions. The 1956 Lahaina case is more informative because it records both the witness’s account and the investigator’s reasoning; its aircraft explanation is plausible, although not proven by a matched flight. The Dan Kimball story is historically influential but less secure than its repetition in UFO literature suggests.

Taken together, the files strengthen the case that Hawaii had a genuine Cold War reporting pattern shaped by military activity, ocean geography and difficult observing conditions. They weaken the more dramatic claim that official records contain persuasive proof of extraordinary craft. Their lasting importance lies in documenting how credible uncertainty entered government channels — and how often the evidence remained too incomplete to establish precisely what had been seen.
