## Introduction

[Montana]({{ 'why-montana-became-a-ufo-landmark/' | relative_url }})’s best-known UFO cases show why “unresolved” is a limited finding, not a verdict that an extraordinary craft was present. Investigators test a claim by separating the event from the interpretation: authenticating records, checking dates and locations, comparing testimony with contemporary documents, measuring what an image can actually reveal, and testing ordinary explanations against the available data.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Montana_8d274f_testing_montana_ufo_78faf6-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Testing Claims" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The 1950 [Great Falls]({{ 'great-falls/' | relative_url }}) film and the 1967 [Malmstrom missile]({{ 'malmstrom/' | relative_url }}) shutdown illustrate opposite evidential problems. The film is a genuine physical record, but its bright, featureless images contain too little information for a secure identification. The missile failure is firmly documented, but the claimed connection to a UFO depends largely on recollections made public decades later and is challenged by the surviving military history. In both cases, the important question is not whether something remained unidentified. It is whether the evidence positively supports unusual technology better than incomplete data, mistaken interpretation or an ordinary event.

## What unidentified does — and does not — mean

In UFO investigation, “unidentified” normally means that the available evidence does not justify a confident identification. It does not establish that every conventional explanation has been eliminated, still less that the object was extraterrestrial. Modern US investigators make the same distinction: cases often remain open because imagery, range information, sensor data or contextual records are insufficient, while many better-documented reports are eventually attributed to balloons, birds, aircraft, drones or sensor effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[aaro.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MUAP ImageryThe video footage associated with this report is insufficient for AARO to render a determination on its subject matter. This matters particularly in Montana because its landmark cases contain real evidence. Nick Mariana’s film exists; the Echo Flight missile shutdown occurred. Those facts raise the cases above unsupported anecdotes, but they prove only the starting points of the respective investigations.

A useful assessment therefore separates at least four questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Did the reported event occur?** A film, maintenance report or official log may establish that something happened.
2. **Is the evidence complete and accurately dated?** Missing frames, uncertain timings and delayed recollections weaken reconstruction.
3. **Can ordinary causes be tested properly?** Aircraft positions, weather, astronomy, equipment faults and witness viewing conditions all matter.
4. **Does the evidence show genuinely anomalous performance?** Claims about extreme speed, deliberate interference or unconventional craft require measurements, not impressions alone.

</div>

A case can pass the first test and fail the remaining three. That is the central lesson of Montana’s evidence.

## How the Great Falls film was tested

The [Mariana film]({{ 'mariana-film/' | relative_url }}) is valuable because it is an original 16 mm colour recording made in 1950 rather than a later reconstruction. It shows two bright images moving across the sky, and Mariana’s secretary also reported seeing the objects. When the University of Colorado study re-examined the case in the 1960s, investigator William Hartmann considered the witnesses broadly credible and concluded that the observation itself had probably occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case47.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCase 47: Great Falls Movie FilmCase 47 Great Falls, Montana (lat. 47° 30&#x27; and long. 111° 18&#x27;) 15 August 1950 (see below) Invest...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1950</span></span></span>

Authenticity, however, is not the same as identification. Analysts had to ask what measurements the footage could support.

### The limits built into the film

The objects are small and lack clear structural detail. Much of the sequence provides no fixed foreground against which their movement can be calibrated. Their distance is unknown, so angular movement across the frame cannot be converted reliably into true speed. A nearby slow object and a distant fast object can create similar image motion. Brightness is also ambiguous: the film cannot by itself show whether the recorded light came from self-luminous objects, sunlight reflected by aircraft or another source.

These limitations prevent several popular claims from being demonstrated. The film does not yield a dependable size, altitude, range or velocity. Nor does it clearly show discs, windows, propulsion systems or manoeuvres beyond known aircraft capability. It records two lights; most of the dramatic physical interpretation comes from witness description rather than recoverable image detail.

The history of the film itself also required checking. Mariana alleged that frames showing clearer disc-like forms had disappeared while the film was in Air Force custody, whereas Air Force personnel maintained that they had removed only a damaged frame. Because the disputed material is unavailable, neither account can now be conclusively tested. The missing-frame issue therefore adds uncertainty rather than positive evidence for a particular craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_UFO_film" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mariana UFO film</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mariana UFO film</span></span></span>

### Testing the aircraft explanation

Early Air Force analysis proposed that the lights were reflections from two F-94 jets. Later reviewers questioned whether the aircraft were in the right position and whether their reflections would have remained as steady as the filmed lights. Project Blue Book ultimately treated the footage as unidentified, while Hartmann’s Colorado study found aircraft difficult to reconcile with the film but not impossible to rule out. His conclusion was deliberately cautious: the case remained unexplained, yet aircraft still represented the principal working hypothesis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_UFO_film" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mariana UFO film</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mariana UFO film</span></span></span>

That conclusion demonstrates how a plausible explanation is tested. Investigators compare the reported direction and time with flight activity, estimate whether sunlight could produce the observed brightness, examine changes in apparent intensity and look for the visual behaviour expected from approaching or receding aircraft. A proposed solution weakens when it conflicts with those details, but rejecting one aircraft scenario does not prove that no aircraft configuration could fit.

The Colorado team also checked details that might appear minor but affect confidence in the reconstruction. Contemporary baseball schedules cast doubt on the long-accepted date of 15 August, because Mariana’s team was away and had no home game during the stated period. The report consequently listed the event as 5 or 15 August 1950. That uncertainty complicates attempts to match the film precisely with weather and aircraft records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case47.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCase 47: Great Falls Movie FilmCase 47 Great Falls, Montana (lat. 47° 30&#x27; and long. 111° 18&#x27;) 15 August 1950 (see below) Invest...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1950</span></span></span>

The strongest fair judgement is therefore narrow: the footage appears genuine and has resisted a conclusive identification, but it contains too little information to establish extraordinary technology. Later study preserved the mystery while weakening claims that the film clearly depicts structured flying discs.

## How the Malmstrom claim is tested

The Malmstrom case begins with a different evidential foundation. The shutdown of all ten Echo Flight Minuteman missile sites on 16 March 1967 is recorded in the 341st Strategic Missile Wing’s command history. The sites lost strategic alert nearly simultaneously, and maintenance teams had to restore them. The technical incident is not in serious dispute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/malmstromufo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black Vault Documentsmalmstromufo.pdf14 Jun 2001 — On 16!-2rch 1967... Rumors of Unidentified Flying Objects (UFO) around the area...</span></span></span>

The disputed step is the claim that a glowing UFO caused the shutdown. Testing that claim requires investigators to establish whether an unusual object was reported at the correct location and time, whether the witnesses had direct knowledge, and whether any physical mechanism connects the sighting to the equipment failures.

### Contemporary records versus later memory

The surviving command history states that rumours of UFO activity around Echo Flight were disproved. It records that a mobile security team operating in the area that morning reported no unusual activity or sightings. That statement does not guarantee that every relevant observation was documented, but it is a near-contemporary record created within the responsible military unit and therefore carries substantial evidential weight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/malmstromufo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault Documents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black Vault Documentsmalmstromufo.pdf14 Jun 2001 — On 16!-2rch 1967... The best-known opposing account comes from former missile officer Robert Salas. He has said that surface guards telephoned an underground launch capsule to report strange lights and then a glowing red object near the gate, shortly before missiles went offline. Salas’s account deserves consideration because he served in the missile force and described operational details consistent with that environment. Yet he did not personally see the reported object from underground, and his UFO account entered public reporting roughly three decades after the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcnews.com/Technology/airmen-govt-clean-ufos/story?id=11738715" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcnews.com">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News Former Airmen to Govt.: Come Clean on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsFormer Airmen to Govt.: Come Clean on UFOsSeptember 27, 2010 — He was stationed 60 feet underground at the Malmstrom Air Force Ba...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 27, 2010</span></span></span>

Delayed testimony is not automatically false. People can retain vivid memories of unusual events for decades. But memory is reconstructive: dates, locations, sequences and information learned later can become blended. Investigators therefore test a retrospective account against duty rosters, unit histories, maintenance messages, security logs and statements recorded nearer the event. Where the later narrative conflicts with contemporary documentation, the conflict must remain visible rather than being resolved solely by judging the witness sincere.

Sincerity and accuracy are separate questions. A witness may honestly remember receiving an alarming telephone report while being mistaken about which flight, date or equipment failure it accompanied. This is especially important at Malmstrom because later accounts have sometimes combined Echo Flight’s documented shutdown with reports associated with another flight or another date.

### The missing causal link

Even if an unusual light was present, coincidence would not establish causation. To show that an object disabled the missiles, investigators would need evidence such as a recorded electromagnetic disturbance, radar or optical tracking that placed the object at the sites, matching fault signatures tied to an external source, or independently timed observations from multiple launch facilities.

The surviving public record establishes simultaneous failures but does not provide that chain. A common technical fault could affect several sites through shared systems without any object in the sky. Conversely, a sighting near a missile area would remain only a parallel event unless its timing and physical effects could be linked to the shutdown.

A 2025 *Wall Street Journal* report said a Pentagon investigation had connected the episode to a classified electromagnetic-pulse test. That claim would offer a conventional mechanism for both secrecy and equipment disruption, but the detailed supporting investigation had not been publicly released in AARO’s historical-report series by early 2026. It should therefore be treated as a significant reported development, not as a fully inspectable public resolution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/ufo-us-disinformation-45376f7e" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wsj.com">[The Wall Street Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wsj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">military deliberately spread disinformation about UFOs, contributing to decades of conspiracy theories. This leaves the evidential balance uneven. The missile failure is strongly documented; the UFO presence is disputed; and the causal connection is weaker still. Later testimony strengthened public awareness of the claim, but the contemporary command history and absence of a demonstrated physical link weaken the argument that an unknown craft caused the shutdown.

## Ordinary explanations and missing data

Montana’s cases show that ordinary explanations need not be proved beyond all doubt to remain viable. They only need to fit the evidence at least as well as the extraordinary claim.

For the Great Falls film, aircraft reflections remain possible because distance and geometry cannot be reconstructed securely. Birds or other small objects are harder to assess for the same reason: the film lacks the resolution, range and calibrated tracking needed to discriminate reliably among alternatives. The result is evidential stalemate, not evidence that conventional objects were impossible.

For Malmstrom, equipment malfunction, a shared electronic disturbance or a classified test all begin with mechanisms known to affect technical systems. The UFO explanation adds an extra proposition — that an unusual craft was present and caused the fault — without a surviving sensor record that demonstrates either step. The evidential burden therefore falls more heavily on the extraordinary interpretation.

Several recurring data gaps prevent stronger conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **No measured range:** apparent size and speed cannot be calculated accurately.
* **No synchronised sensors:** visual reports cannot be matched securely with radar, film or equipment telemetry.
* **Uncertain chronology:** even small errors in date or time can break a proposed link to aircraft, weather or system faults.
* **Incomplete archival survival:** missing logs, unavailable film frames and classified material limit later review.
* **Retrospective testimony:** memories collected decades later may add detail while reducing confidence in exact sequence and attribution.

</div>

Modern scientific proposals for studying unusual aerial events aim to close precisely these gaps. They call for multiple calibrated instruments measuring position, spectrum, radar range, sound and environmental conditions at the same time. Triangulation can establish distance; independent sensors can distinguish a physical object from a camera artefact; and precise timestamps allow comparison with aircraft, satellites and weather.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

Neither Montana landmark case was recorded with that level of instrumentation. Investigators are consequently trying to reconstruct three-dimensional events from a short film in one case and from technical records plus later memories in the other.

## When does an unresolved case mean something?

An unresolved Montana case is meaningful when the underlying report is authentic, the witnesses or records are credible, and obvious explanations do not fit cleanly. The Mariana film meets much of that standard: it is historically important evidence of an object or objects that could not be identified confidently from the available imagery.

Meaningful does not mean extraordinary. A case becomes strong evidence for unusual technology only when it contains reliable measurements of genuinely anomalous characteristics — for example, independently confirmed acceleration, physical effects tied directly to the object, or detailed imagery that excludes known aircraft and natural phenomena. Neither the Mariana film nor the publicly available Malmstrom evidence reaches that threshold.

The two cases instead preserve different kinds of uncertainty. Great Falls is an image-rich but information-poor case: investigators can repeatedly examine the film, yet cannot recover the missing distance and geometry. Malmstrom is a record-rich but linkage-poor case: documents confirm a serious missile failure, yet do not securely connect it with a UFO.

That distinction is more useful than simply labelling both cases “unexplained”. Montana’s evidence shows that a mystery may survive careful investigation because the decisive data were never collected, not because investigators discovered proof of an extraordinary craft.
