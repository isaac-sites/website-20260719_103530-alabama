## Introduction

Two episodes dominate the record. The July 1952 Washington radar sightings involved controllers, pilots and Andrews Air Force Base, placing Maryland inside one of America’s most consequential UFO “flaps”. Six years later, the [Loch Raven]({{ 'loch-raven/' | relative_url }}) Reservoir incident became Maryland’s clearest standalone case when two motorists reported a hovering object, a disabled car and physical heat. The Air Force left that case unidentified, but the surviving evidence is still too limited to establish what the object was. More recent drone scares show the same recurring problem: an initially extraordinary report can weaken sharply once stars, aircraft and lawful drones are considered.

## Why Maryland became a natural UFO crossroads

Maryland sits beneath unusually complicated skies. The Baltimore–Washington region contains major commercial flight paths, restricted airspace, military installations and federal research centres. Andrews Air Force Base, now Joint Base Andrews, lies just inside Maryland; Naval Air Station Patuxent River is a major flight-test centre; Baltimore/Washington International Airport handles dense civil traffic; and NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center is based in Greenbelt.

That geography does not prove that Maryland should experience more genuinely anomalous events. It does, however, increase the number of unfamiliar things residents may see: military aircraft, helicopters, test flights, airliners turning towards airports, satellites, balloons and, increasingly, drones. It also means that sightings near Maryland can quickly acquire national-security significance, particularly when they occur close to Washington or sensitive facilities.

## The 1952 Washington flap and Maryland’s radar connection

The most important UFO episode connected with Maryland occurred over the Washington region on two successive weekends in July 1952. Air-traffic controllers at Washington National Airport reported unexplained radar returns, while personnel at Andrews Air Force Base in Maryland also described unusual lights or radar targets. Fighter aircraft were scrambled, press attention became intense and the incident helped force the Air Force into one of the largest public briefings of the early UFO era.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Washington%2C_D.C._UFO_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1952 Washington, D.C. The case matters because it was not simply a lone person seeing a light. Reports came from several professional observers and involved more than one radar facility. Some pilots described lights that appeared to correspond with targets seen by controllers. Andrews personnel gave visual accounts of orange or white lights, although other observations during the same nights were attributed to stars or meteors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Washington%2C_D.C._UFO_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1952 Washington, D.C. UFO incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1952 Washington, D.C. UFO incident</span></span></span>

The evidence was nevertheless less decisive than later retellings often suggest. Radar in 1952 was vulnerable to anomalous propagation, in which layers of warm and cool air bend radio waves and create misleading returns. The Air Force publicly emphasised temperature inversions and visual misidentification. Critics of that explanation noted that some controllers regarded the returns as stronger and more coherent than typical weather effects, and that not every visual report could be connected neatly to a radar artefact. A surviving CIA-era summary records that the inversion explanation began as a suggestion from an Air Technical Intelligence Center official rather than as a single conclusive reconstruction of every observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP81R00560R000100010002-9.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FLYING SAUCERS UFO REPORTSIt was a chance remark by an ATIC official, sug- gesting that the radar targets might have been caused by te...</span></span></span>

The fairest assessment is that the Washington flap combined several things: genuine radar anomalies, astronomical objects, possible meteors, ordinary aircraft and perhaps observations that cannot now be reconstructed. Its lasting significance for Maryland lies in Andrews’ participation and in the episode’s demonstration that unidentified objects near the capital could trigger immediate military, intelligence and public concern. It remains unresolved in parts, but the evidence does not justify the common claim that radar proved structured craft were manoeuvring over Washington.

## Loch Raven: Maryland’s landmark close encounter

Maryland’s best-known independent case took place near Loch Raven Reservoir, north of Baltimore, at about 10.30 pm on 26 October 1958. Two motorists, later identified as Philip Small and Alvin Cohen, said they saw a large, flat, egg-shaped object hovering above a bridge. As they drove closer, they reported that the car’s engine, headlights and dashboard lights stopped simultaneously.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/docs/581026lochravendam_docs1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">581026lochravendam docs1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">581026lochravendam docs1</span></span></span>

According to the Air Force case summary, the men left the vehicle and watched from behind it. They described a brilliant white flash, a loud noise and heat on their faces, followed by the object rising vertically and disappearing within seconds. Once it had gone, the car reportedly restarted normally. The men reached a telephone about 15 minutes later, contacted Towson police and were interviewed by officers. They later went to hospital because of a burning sensation, although the file records no lasting injury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/docs/581026lochravendam_docs1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">581026lochravendam docs1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">581026lochravendam docs1</span></span></span>

### What the case does not prove

Loch Raven is intriguing, but its evidential limits are substantial. There are no photographs, instrument readings, recovered materials or independent witnesses known to have seen the object at close range. The estimated size and altitude came from frightened observers viewing an unfamiliar luminous object at night, circumstances in which distance can be very difficult to judge.

The reported electrical failure is central to the story, yet the surviving documentation does not establish a mechanism linking the object to the car. A stalled engine and extinguished lights would be unusual if independently verified by mechanical inspection, but the record relies mainly on witness testimony. Similarly, the sensation of heat is recorded, but there is no clear evidence of burns or measurable radiation.

Possible conventional explanations include an unusual aircraft, a bright astronomical object badly misjudged in distance, a meteor or electrical fault combined with fear. None fits every reported detail comfortably. That is why “unidentified” remains reasonable. It is not equivalent to “alien spacecraft”; it means the information preserved in the file does not support a confident identification.

## Flaps, clusters and the limits of sighting databases

Maryland has accumulated many later reports of triangles, discs, fireballs and formations of lights. The National UFO Reporting Center’s public index contains nearly two thousand Maryland entries, but this figure is a count of submitted reports, not a count of verified anomalous craft. Entries are largely self-reported, vary greatly in detail and may include aircraft, satellites, planets, meteors, drones and hoaxes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/ndx/?id=loc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reports by Location NUFORC Reports by Location; USA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUFORC Reports by LocationNUFORC Reports by Location; USA - Louisiana, 1235; USA - Massachusetts, 2898; USA - Maryland, 1990; U...</span></span></span>

Clusters often reflect where people live, where air traffic is busiest and when media coverage encourages more reporting. A wave of stories can improve vigilance, but it can also create expectation: once people are looking for “mystery objects”, ordinary lights become more likely to be interpreted through that frame.

For Maryland researchers, the most useful reports are therefore not necessarily the most spectacular. A case gains value when it includes a precise time and location, independent witnesses, photographs with original metadata, air-traffic information, weather records or corroboration from radar. A dramatic narrative without such material is generally weaker than a modest report supported by several independent data sources.

## The 2024 drone scare as a modern cautionary case

Maryland became part of the wider north-eastern drone panic in December 2024. Former governor Larry Hogan posted video from Davidsonville and said he had watched what appeared to be dozens of large drones. The claim attracted attention because similar reports were spreading through New Jersey, New York and neighbouring states.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://x.com/GovLarryHogan/status/1867608947525386534" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: x.com">[X (formerly Twitter)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">x.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on x.com.</span></span></span>

Astronomers and other observers quickly pointed out that prominent lights in the footage matched stars in Orion. This did not prove that every light Hogan saw was a star, but it substantially weakened the video as evidence of a large formation of unidentified drones. The episode is a useful Maryland example of how an apparently credible witness can make an honest error when bright celestial objects are viewed without a reliable reference for distance or movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_States_drone_sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2024 United States drone sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2024 United States drone sightings</span></span></span>

Federal investigators received more than 5,000 reports during the wider East Coast episode, but fewer than 100 initially merited detailed follow-up. Officials said the available evidence pointed largely to lawful drones, crewed aircraft and misidentification, with no indication of a large hostile operation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/world/us/what-we-know-about-us-drone-sightings-causing-an-online-frenzy-2024-12-16/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on reuters.com.</span></span></span>

This does not make [drone incursions]({{ 'drone-incursions/' | relative_url }}) unimportant. Small uncrewed aircraft near airports, bases or critical infrastructure are genuine safety and security concerns. It does show why “unidentified” reports should not be grouped automatically into one extraordinary phenomenon. Some may involve real drones; some are aircraft seen head-on; some are stars or planets; and a residue may remain unresolved because the original evidence is inadequate.

## Military secrecy, archives and persistent suspicion

Maryland’s military geography encourages claims that hidden UFO programmes or recovered craft are housed at local installations, particularly Patuxent River. Publicly available evidence does not substantiate those claims. The existence of classified flight testing can explain why witnesses sometimes see unfamiliar aircraft or why officials cannot discuss every activity, but secrecy by itself is not evidence of extraterrestrial technology.

The Department of Defense’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office reviewed historical claims of concealed UFO recovery and reverse-engineering programmes. Its 2024 report said it found no empirical evidence that the United States government or private companies had possessed or reverse-engineered extraterrestrial technology. It also concluded that most resolved sightings involved ordinary objects, natural phenomena or misidentification, while many unresolved cases lacked enough researchable data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/AARO_Historical_Record_Report_Vol_1_2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Unclassified Final DSD AARO Historical Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unclassified Final DSD AARO Historical Report</span></span></span>

That conclusion should not be treated as proof that every Maryland sighting has been solved. Official inquiries can be incomplete, and some historical files are frustratingly thin. It does, however, place the burden of proof on claims about hidden vehicles or alien materials. Without documents, physical evidence or independently verifiable testimony, such stories remain allegations rather than established parts of Maryland’s UFO history.

The strongest archival material remains the Project Blue Book record. ## How strong is Maryland’s UFO evidence?

Maryland has one genuinely notable close-encounter file, an important supporting role in the 1952 Washington radar incidents and a long tail of poorly documented reports. Taken together, this is a significant state history, but not a persuasive body of evidence for extraterrestrial visitation.

The cases fall into three practical categories:

**Unresolved but evidentially interesting:** Loch Raven belongs here. The witnesses reported quickly, police became involved and the Air Force investigated. The absence of a solution is meaningful, but the lack of photographs, measurements and physical evidence prevents a firm conclusion.

**Historically important but probably mixed:** The 1952 Washington flap involved credible observers and genuine radar concern, yet it almost certainly combined several causes. Weather-related radar effects, stars, meteors and aircraft explain part of the episode, while some observations remain uncertain.

**Weak or later diminished:** Most isolated lights and many modern drone claims fall into this group. They may have been sincerely reported, but insufficient data or later identification as aircraft, stars, satellites or lawful drones reduces their value.

NASA’s modern position captures the central lesson. UAP research is limited less by a shortage of stories than by poor-quality data. Useful investigation requires calibrated instruments, exact timing, reliable distance estimates and multiple independent forms of observation. Without those, even an extraordinary account may remain permanently unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nasa.gov.</span></span></span>

## What Maryland’s record ultimately shows

Maryland’s UFO history is not a catalogue of confirmed exotic craft. It is a record of how ambiguous events are experienced and investigated in one of America’s most sensitive and crowded air corridors.

Loch Raven remains the state’s most compelling mystery because the account was immediate, detailed and formally investigated. The Washington radar flap matters because Maryland’s Andrews base helped turn a regional air-traffic puzzle into a national controversy. The 2024 drone episode matters for the opposite reason: it demonstrates how quickly public alarm can grow around ordinary stars, aircraft and drones.

The enduring pattern is therefore not proof of a single hidden phenomenon. It is the repeated collision of unusual observations, incomplete data, aviation complexity, military secrecy and human perception. Some Maryland cases deserve to remain open. None currently provides verified evidence of extraterrestrial origin.
