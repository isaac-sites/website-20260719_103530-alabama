## Introduction

The best-known images of the [Phoenix Lights]({{ 'phoenix-lights/' | relative_url }}) do not show the earlier formation of lights that travelled across Arizona. Instead, they show a later event that appeared around 10pm on 13 March 1997: a row of bright lights seen south-west of Phoenix that seemed to hover motionless and then disappear one by one. This later display is the part of the Phoenix Lights most strongly explained by military illumination flares. Multiple investigations concluded that aircraft operating on a training exercise dropped long-burning parachute flares over the Barry [Goldwater Range]({{ 'goldwater-range/' | relative_url }}), a vast military training area south-west of the city. Witnesses saw the lights from tens of miles away, beyond the Estrella Mountains, creating visual effects that made the flares appear mysterious and stationary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

## When and Where the Later Lights Appeared

A key point often lost in retellings is that the famous videos were recorded during a second event, not the earlier formation reported moving across [Arizona]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-arizonas-ufo/' | relative_url }}). Around 10pm, observers in the Phoenix area saw a line or arc of bright lights in the south-western sky. Unlike the earlier reports, these lights appeared largely stationary. Numerous cameras captured them, making this the most widely documented portion of the Phoenix Lights story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

The US Air Force later stated that A-10 Thunderbolt II aircraft participating in [Operation Snowbird]({{ 'snowbird-flights/' | relative_url }}) training exercises had dropped LUU-2B/B illumination flares over the Barry Goldwater Range. The aircraft involved were associated with the Maryland Air National Guard, temporarily operating from Davis-Monthan Air Force Base near Tucson. Reports linking the lights to these flare exercises emerged within months of the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

The geographical setting matters. The flare drops occurred far from central Phoenix. From many viewing locations, the lights appeared above or beyond the Estrella mountain range on the horizon. At night, observers could not easily judge distance, altitude or the shape of the terrain between themselves and the lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/arts-culture/the-phoenix-lights-are-no-mystery-6661825/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phoenixnewtimes.com">[phoenixnewtimes.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phoenixnewtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the phoenix lights are no mystery 6661825</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix New TimesThe 1997 &quot;Phoenix Lights&quot; are no mystery14 Mar 2014 — The “disappearance” was actually the lights dropping behind the Si...</span></span></span>

## How Parachute Flares Can Seem to Hover

Military illumination flares are designed to provide light over a target area. After release, a parachute deploys and slows the flare's descent. Rather than streaking rapidly across the sky like a meteor, the flare can remain visible for several minutes while descending gradually. Seen from a great distance, most of that motion is directed away from the observer rather than sideways across the field of view. The result is an object that appears almost motionless.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

* The lights remained in roughly the same area of sky.
* They appeared bright and steady rather than rapidly manoeuvring.
* Individual lights faded separately rather than moving away together.
* The display lasted for a period consistent with long-burning illumination flares.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

Witnesses often interpreted the lights as belonging to a single enormous object. However, human depth perception is poor at night when there are few visual reference points. Separate lights at great distance can appear connected, especially when arranged in a loose geometric pattern. ## Why the Lights Disappeared One by One

The strongest piece of evidence for the flare explanation is the way the lights vanished.

Videos show the lights extinguishing individually rather than all at once. To many observers, this looked as though a gigantic craft was turning off its lights. Investigators reached a different conclusion: as the flares descended under parachutes, they gradually dropped behind the ridgeline of the Estrella Mountains. ## What the Videos Show and What They Cannot Prove

The videos are important evidence, but they have limits.

They clearly show bright lights in the night sky. They do not show a structured craft connecting those lights. The cameras used in 1997 lacked the resolution needed to reveal detailed shapes at such distances, especially under low-light conditions. As a result, the recordings demonstrate that lights were present, but they cannot establish the size, distance or physical nature of an object behind them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

This distinction is crucial because many later documentaries and television programmes used footage of the 10pm lights while discussing witness reports from the earlier [moving formation]({{ 'moving-formation/' | relative_url }}). Combining the two events can create the impression that the famous videos documented a giant V-shaped craft crossing Arizona. The available evidence suggests otherwise: the widely circulated footage primarily records the later stationary display that closely matches military flare activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

## Why the Flare Explanation Remains Central to the Debate

Within Arizona's UFO history, the flare explanation is significant because it addresses the most famous visual evidence associated with the Phoenix Lights. Even some researchers who remain unconvinced about every aspect of the evening's events accept that the later lights are much easier to explain than the earlier reports of a moving formation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

The debate persists largely because eyewitness testimony and video evidence point in different directions. Many witnesses sincerely believed they were observing something extraordinary. Yet the combination of documented military exercises, the location of the flare range, the behaviour of parachute flares, and the characteristic one-by-one disappearance behind the Estrella Mountains provides a coherent conventional explanation for the later display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>

For that reason, the later Phoenix Lights are often regarded as one of the strongest examples in Arizona UFO history of how a dramatic night-time sighting can be transformed by distance, perspective and the difficulty of judging lights in a dark sky. The flare explanation does not resolve every report from 13 March 1997, but it substantially explains the specific event captured in the famous 10pm videos.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Phoenix Lights</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phoenix Lights</span></span></span>
