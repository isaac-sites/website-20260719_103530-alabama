## The 1897 airship wave: Arkansas before flying saucers

Arkansas’s earliest famous UFO stories appeared decades before the term “flying saucer”. During 1896 and 1897, newspapers across the United States carried reports of mysterious illuminated “airships”. The wave began in California, spread through the Midwest and reached Arkansas in the spring of 1897. Witnesses generally imagined an unknown human inventor rather than visitors from another planet, reflecting an age fascinated by machinery, balloons and the possibility of powered flight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.readex.com/blog/ufo-fever-americas-historical-newspapers-mysterious-airships" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: readex.com">[readex.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">readex.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo fever americas historical newspapers mysterious airships</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mysterious Airships of 1896-9712 Sept 2014 — For five months beginning in November 1896, newspapers across the country described stra...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1896</span></span></span>

The Arkansas reports grew rapidly more elaborate. On 17 April, J. F. Floyd of Texarkana reportedly saw what looked like a great ball of fire travelling at high speed. Three days later, Iron Mountain Railroad conductor Jim Hooton told the *Arkansas Gazette* that he had encountered a landed airship while hunting near Homan in Miller County. He described machinery sounding like a railway air brake and claimed to have spoken with crew members who said their craft used compressed air.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com/2015/08/ufos-and-arkansas.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com">[arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">From the Vault: Arkansas History&#x27;s Mysteries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs and Arkansas...</span></span></span>

### What probably drove the reports?

Several explanations can coexist. Isolated observers may have seen bright planets, meteors, balloons or distant lights and interpreted them through the airship stories filling local newspapers. Some witnesses may have invented encounters for amusement or publicity. Experimental lighter-than-air craft existed, but no known machine of the period convincingly matches the long journeys and dramatic performances described in the Arkansas accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com/2015/08/ufos-and-arkansas.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com">[arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">From the Vault: Arkansas History&#x27;s Mysteries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs and Arkansas...</span></span></span>

The most defensible conclusion is that the wave combined misidentification, expectation, folklore and deliberate joking. It remains historically important because it shows how UFO-like narratives can form before modern alien imagery. Arkansas residents did not require spacecraft from another world to create a flap; they interpreted ambiguous sights through the technological hopes and newspaper culture of their own time.

## The 1965 sightings: Arkansas’s strongest UFO flap

Arkansas’s most important modern wave occurred in August 1965. Unlike the 1897 stories, these reports belonged to the flying-saucer era and attracted police attention, organised camera watches and interest from the US Air Force’s Project Blue Book. The activity centred particularly on Washington County and Fort Smith, though reports also came from other parts of the state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/ufo-sightings-8576/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopediaofarkansas.net">[Encyclopedia of Arkansas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopediaofarkansas.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Encyclopedia of Arkansas UFO Sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Encyclopedia of ArkansasUFO Sightings - Encyclopedia of Arkansas...</span></span></span>

On 4 August, Bill Estep reported a flashing light near [Viney Grove]({{ 'viney-grove/' | relative_url }}). After approaching the area, he described a long, narrow silver object with illuminated windows and a rotating light hovering just above the trees. Prairie Grove police officer Rubin Strong could not confirm the object, but reportedly believed Estep had seen something. Lights were also reported around Fayetteville that night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/ufo-sightings-8576/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopediaofarkansas.net">[Encyclopedia of Arkansas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopediaofarkansas.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Encyclopedia of Arkansas UFO Sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Encyclopedia of ArkansasUFO Sightings - Encyclopedia of Arkansas...</span></span></span>

The distinction matters: believing that Estep saw something is not the same as verifying his description. Without photographs, measured distance, reliable angular size or a second close observer, an apparently structured object could have been a distant aircraft, a bright astronomical source distorted by atmosphere, or several lights visually combined into a single form. Perception at night is particularly vulnerable to errors in judging distance and movement because the observer often lacks fixed reference points.

Contemporary newspaper reporting itself acknowledged the evidential weakness. One *Arkansas Gazette* headline stated that UFOs had been reported in northern Arkansas but that cameras had captured none. The absence of photographs does not prove that nothing unusual occurred, but it weakened claims of a persistent, repeatedly visible craft. It also meant that investigators had to rely largely on changing descriptions and retrospective testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/media/ufo-article-12371/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopediaofarkansas.net">[Encyclopedia of Arkansas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopediaofarkansas.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Encyclopedia of ArkansasUFO SightingsArticle about UFO sightings in northern Arkansas from the Arkansas Gazette. &quot;UFOs are reported in no...</span></span></span>

## Fort Smith and the claim of 1,500 witnesses

The best-known part of the 1965 wave involved Fort Smith. Residents reported red, green and white lights in the sky, with crowds gathering at different points around the city. Contemporary material describes groups numbering from dozens to hundreds, and a Project Blue Book record was later said to estimate that as many as 1,500 people saw the phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://localtvkfsm.files.wordpress.com/2015/01/ufo.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: localtvkfsm.files.wordpress.com">[KFSM Local TV]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">localtvkfsm.files.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KFSM Local TVproject 10073 recordJanuary 8, 2015 — 1 Jan 2015 — THESE TEEN-AGE BOYS point to the. IJid!s over Fort Smith after spotting a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 8, 2015</span></span></span>

That figure makes the case sound exceptionally strong, but crowd size must be interpreted cautiously. Fifteen hundred people did not necessarily provide 1,500 independent, consistent observations. Many may have watched the same distant lights after being alerted by radio, neighbours or gathered spectators. Once a public watch begins, social expectation influences where people look and how they describe ambiguous lights. A large crowd confirms that a public event occurred; it does not by itself establish extraordinary flight characteristics.

The preserved reporting mentions coloured lights rather than a clearly observed solid craft. Red, green and white are also standard aircraft navigation and anti-collision colours. Depending on viewing angle, an aircraft flying towards an observer can appear nearly stationary, while atmospheric haze can obscure its body and leave only its lights visible. Stars and planets can also appear to flicker or change colour when low in the sky. These possibilities do not prove that every Fort Smith report had the same cause, but they substantially reduce the need for an exotic explanation.

Project Blue Book’s involvement is significant because it places the episode within an official archive rather than leaving it solely in UFO literature. Blue Book was the Air Force programme responsible for collecting and evaluating reports from 1952 until 1969. Its surviving files are held by the US National Archives, including chronologically arranged case records, photographs and investigative material. Across the programme as a whole, 12,618 reports were logged and 701 remained classified as unidentified. The Air Force stated that it found no evidence that the unexplained cases represented extraterrestrial vehicles or technology beyond contemporary scientific knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

“Unidentified” in this context does not mean “alien”. It usually means that investigators lacked enough reliable information to reach a defensible identification. A distant light reported by many people can remain officially unresolved because weather records, flight paths, astronomical positions or accurate bearings were unavailable—not because investigators established impossible performance.

Later Arkansas accounts sometimes place the Fort Smith flap in 1966 rather than 1965. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas and its cited newspaper sources place the major reporting in August 1965, while some modern retellings refer to 1966 or describe a broader 1965–66 period. This date drift is a warning sign when using later summaries: the basic incident is well attested, but details have become blurred through repeated retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/ufo-sightings-8576/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: encyclopediaofarkansas.net">[Encyclopedia of Arkansas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">encyclopediaofarkansas.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Encyclopedia of Arkansas UFO Sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Encyclopedia of ArkansasUFO Sightings - Encyclopedia of Arkansas...</span></span></span>

## Military and aviation connections

Arkansas contains several locations that naturally encourage speculation about military activity, including Little Rock Air Force Base and Fort Chaffee. Military aircraft, training flights, flares and restricted operations can create unfamiliar sights, especially at night. They can also make later witnesses suspect secrecy when officials provide limited information.

Yet Arkansas’s best-known cases do not come with strong published evidence of radar confirmation, intercepted unknown aircraft or a documented scramble by military pilots. The Fort Smith reports reached Project Blue Book, but public summaries emphasise witness numbers and lights rather than matched radar tracks or recorded manoeuvres beyond known aircraft capability. That makes the case less technically compelling than famous national incidents involving multiple calibrated sensors.

A military location nearby is therefore context, not proof. Conventional training can generate reports, while genuine uncertainty may persist because operational details are unavailable. Conversely, the inability to identify a light should not automatically be converted into a classified-aircraft explanation. Without flight logs, radar records or precise timing, “secret military activity” is as unverified as an extraterrestrial claim.

Modern defence investigations follow the same basic distinction. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office lists aircraft, balloons, birds, drones, satellites, airborne debris, optical effects and sensor artefacts among common sources of UAP reports. It also recognises that unidentified activity near sensitive airspace can matter as a safety or security issue even when there is no evidence of alien technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

## What Arkansas sighting databases can—and cannot—show

Civilian reporting organisations continue to collect Arkansas sightings. The National UFO Reporting Center’s state index contains more than a thousand reports associated with Arkansas, including lights, triangles, fireballs, circles and formations. Such databases are useful for locating witness narratives and identifying possible clusters, but they are not confirmed catalogues of unexplained craft. Reports are generally submitted by members of the public and vary sharply in detail, timeliness and supporting evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/subndx/?id=lAR" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nuforc.org.</span></span></span>

Raw totals can also be misleading. Cities with larger populations, darker surrounding countryside, active local media or greater awareness of reporting websites may produce more submissions. A place with many reports is not necessarily a physical hotspot. It may simply contain more observers or more people willing to file accounts.

Delayed reporting is another problem. One NUFORC entry describing a supposed Fort Smith event in the mid-1960s was submitted in 2010, more than four decades later. Such recollections can be sincere, but precise dates, durations, crowd estimates and object descriptions are vulnerable to memory changes and influence from later UFO narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/sighting/?id=74083" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nuforc.org.</span></span></span>

Modern technology has created new recurring explanations. Long lines of Starlink satellites have repeatedly been mistaken for UFO formations across the Ozarks and in Arkansas television coverage. Their unusual appearance—many equally spaced lights travelling silently along the same path—can be startling to observers who have not seen a recently launched satellite “train”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ky3.com/2021/05/07/not-a-ufo-what-last-nights-line-of-lights-in-the-ozarks-actually-means/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ky3.com">[https://www.ky3.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ky3.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ky3.com.</span></span></span>

Drones add another layer. A hovering light that would once have suggested a helicopter, balloon or celestial object may now be a small remotely operated aircraft. At the same time, drones can present real aviation and security concerns. Correctly identifying an object as terrestrial does not make every report trivial.

## Witness credibility versus evidential strength

Arkansas UFO history includes police officers, railway workers, radio operators and large groups of ordinary residents. Their social standing may make deliberate fabrication less likely in individual cases, but credibility has limits. Honest witnesses can misjudge size, speed and distance, particularly at night. A light with no visible background can appear to accelerate when the observer moves, and several separate lights can be perceived as belonging to one large object.

The 1897 law-enforcement witnesses are a good example. Their positions gave the Hot Springs story authority, yet the account contains no recoverable physical evidence and belongs to a wave full of jokes and copycat reports. The 1965 police response near Viney Grove similarly supports the view that the witness was taken seriously, but the officer did not independently confirm the reported craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com/2015/08/ufos-and-arkansas.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com">[arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arkansasstatearchives.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">From the Vault: Arkansas History&#x27;s Mysteries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs and Arkansas...</span></span></span>

## Why Arkansas still matters in UFO history

Arkansas offers an unusually clear comparison between two different eras of aerial mystery. In 1897, witnesses saw “airships” shaped by expectations about inventors, compressed air and mechanical flight. In 1965, similar uncertainty was expressed through the language of silver craft, hovering objects and flying saucers. Today, the same process involves drones, satellites and UAP terminology.

The state’s history also demonstrates how flaps develop. An initial report receives publicity; more people begin watching the sky; ambiguous lights attract attention they would otherwise never receive; investigators and journalists arrive; and later memories merge separate observations into a single dramatic event. This does not mean every witness is mistaken or that nothing unusual was present. It means the reporting environment becomes part of the phenomenon.

The 1897 encounters are best classified as culturally important but evidentially weak, with hoaxing, humour and misidentification all strongly plausible. The 1965 north-west Arkansas and Fort Smith wave is better documented and included official interest, but surviving public evidence does not establish a structured craft with extraordinary capabilities. Modern sighting reports remain valuable as leads, particularly where aviation safety may be involved, but most lack the sensor data needed for firm conclusions.

Arkansas’s most durable UFO story is therefore not one of confirmed visitors. It is the repeated collision between unfamiliar sights, credible human uncertainty, changing technology and the stories each generation uses to explain the sky.
