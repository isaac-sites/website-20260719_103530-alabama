The short answer is no: the surviving evidence does not show that radar conclusively tracked the same object seen by witnesses at [Redmond Airport]({{ 'redmond-airport/' | relative_url }}) on 24 September 1959. ## What the FAA and military radar records actually reported

The Redmond case unfolded during the early morning hours when police officer Robert Dickerson and later Federal Aviation Agency personnel observed an unusual bright light. Because the report came from airport personnel, it quickly entered the air-defence reporting network and attracted military attention. Fighter aircraft were directed towards central [Oregon]({{ 'which-oregon-ufo-cases-still-resist/' | relative_url }}), and military radar facilities were asked to check for unusual traffic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/590924redmond_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">590924redmond dir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportThe Air Force radar at Klamath Falls tracked the disc. Given the Air Force &quot;explanations&quot; and some incorrect data in 1959...</span></span></span>

The key point is that the radar evidence did not originate from the airport observers themselves. The visual witnesses were looking at a bright light near the horizon. The radar return was being reported by military radar operators at a separate facility. In later retellings these elements were often merged into a single narrative, creating the impression that controllers were watching the same object both visually and on radar. The surviving Blue Book-era material does not clearly demonstrate that connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/590924redmond_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">590924redmond dir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportThe Air Force radar at Klamath Falls tracked the disc. Researchers who examined the case files found references to a radar contact being followed for a period of time, but the available records do not contain the kind of detailed radar plots, scope photographs or tracking data that would be needed to establish a direct match between the visual sighting and the radar return. As a result, there is no reliable reconstruction showing that the radar target occupied the same position, altitude or flight path as the luminous object seen from Redmond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/590924redmond_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">590924redmond dir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportThe Air Force radar at Klamath Falls tracked the disc. ## Why the Air Force identified the return as a fixed mountain installation

The most important development came during the Air Force investigation. Blue Book personnel eventually concluded that the radar return was probably not an airborne object at all. According to the official explanation, the radar operators were repeatedly detecting a fixed installation on a mountain rather than a moving craft. The radar contact was therefore treated as a separate issue from the visual sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/590924redmond_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">590924redmond dir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportThe Air Force radar at Klamath Falls tracked the disc. Given the Air Force &quot;explanations&quot; and some incorrect data in 1959...</span></span></span>

This conclusion rested on a known limitation of primary radar. Radar systems of the late 1950s could generate returns from terrain, elevated structures and other fixed features, particularly under certain propagation conditions. A primary radar display simply showed reflected energy and could not automatically distinguish between an aircraft and every other reflecting object in its coverage area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport_surveillance_radar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airport surveillance radar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airport surveillance radar</span></span></span>

Critics of the Air Force explanation have argued that radar operators should have recognised a stationary mountain target and that the return was reportedly treated as unusual at the time. Supporters of the official interpretation respond that radar anomalies and misidentified ground returns were not uncommon, especially in an era before modern digital processing and automated target discrimination. Because the original radar data are incomplete, neither side can fully test its case today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/590924redmond_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">590924redmond dir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportThe Air Force radar at Klamath Falls tracked the disc. Given the Air Force &quot;explanations&quot; and some incorrect data in 1959...</span></span></span>

What is clear from the surviving record is that the Air Force did not ultimately treat the radar contact as confirmation of the object observed from Redmond Airport. Instead, investigators separated the two events and offered different explanations for each.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/590924redmond_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">590924redmond dir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportThe Air Force radar at Klamath Falls tracked the disc. ## How missing logs limit claims about altitude, speed and pursuit

Many dramatic retellings of the Redmond incident describe a radar-tracked object racing across Oregon while military aircraft attempted an interception. The surviving documentation does not support such precise claims.

To establish a genuine radar track, investigators would normally want information such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Time-stamped radar plots.
* Measured bearings and ranges.
* Successive position reports.
* Estimated speed calculations.
* Altitude information where available.
* Recorded communications between radar operators and intercept aircraft.

</div>

For the Redmond case, much of that material is either missing, incomplete or unavailable in the surviving public record. Without those data, later writers cannot reliably calculate how fast the target moved, how high it was, or whether it manoeuvred in a way that ruled out ordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fold3.com/publication/461/us-project-blue-book-ufo-investigations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fold3.com">[Fold3]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fold3.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">US, Project Blue Book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">US, Project Blue Book - UFO Investigations, 1947-196926 Feb 2007 — Records and case files relating to investigations of sightings of...</span></span></span>

This limitation is especially important because some modern summaries present the radar return as if it were a fully documented pursuit. In reality, there is no surviving chain of evidence showing a fighter aircraft locking onto the same target seen by airport observers, nor a radar record demonstrating extraordinary performance characteristics. ## Why the radar question remains unresolved

The radar issue sits at the heart of why the Redmond case remains debated within Oregon UFO history. If the visual sighting and radar return represented one object, the incident would stand as a stronger example of multiple independent forms of evidence converging on the same event. If they were separate occurrences, the case becomes more ambiguous.

The surviving record leans towards the second interpretation. Air Force investigators treated the radar contact and the visual observation as different phenomena, attributing the radar return to a fixed mountain installation while continuing to wrestle with the visual reports. At the same time, gaps in the documentation prevent a definitive reconstruction. The missing radar logs mean that modern researchers cannot completely verify or completely dismiss the claimed connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/590924redmond_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">590924redmond dir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportThe Air Force radar at Klamath Falls tracked the disc.
