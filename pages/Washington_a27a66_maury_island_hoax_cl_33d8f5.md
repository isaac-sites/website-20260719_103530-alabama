## Introduction

The Maury Island story is one of [Washington]({{ 'why-washington-became-americas-first-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s most dramatic early UFO cases, but the surviving evidence strongly favours a hoax rather than an unexplained aerial encounter. Harold Dahl and Fred Crisman claimed that six doughnut-shaped craft appeared near Maury Island in Puget Sound on 21 June 1947, with one dropping metal sheets and hot rock-like debris onto Dahl’s boat. The alleged fall supposedly damaged the vessel, injured a boy and killed a dog. Dahl also described photographs and a threatening stranger in a dark suit.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Washington_a27a66_maury_island_hoax_cl_33d8f5-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Maury Island" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What made the affair famous was not strong physical evidence, but what happened afterwards. [Kenneth Arnold]({{ 'kenneth-arnold/' | relative_url }}) became involved, two military intelligence officers examined the story, and both officers died hours later when their B-25 crashed near Kelso. That genuine tragedy became attached to claims of secret cargo, sabotage and government suppression. Yet the photographs disappeared, the debris appeared ordinary, important details proved false or contradictory, and official investigators concluded that Dahl and Crisman had manufactured the central story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylink.org/File/2068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylink.org">[HistoryLink]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylink.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dahl and Crissman report a June 21, 1947, explosion of a flying saucer over Maury Island on or after June 26, 1947. - HistoryL...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 1947</span></span></span>

## Dahl and Crisman's original claims

Dahl said that he was operating a boat near Maury Island with his teenage son, two crewmen and a dog when six large, circular objects appeared beneath a low cloud layer. Five allegedly circled a sixth object that seemed to be in difficulty. According to the later published account, the damaged object released thin, light-coloured metal followed by heavier black fragments resembling lava rock or slag. Some of the material supposedly struck the boat, hurt Dahl’s son and killed the dog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylink.org/File/2068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylink.org">[HistoryLink]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylink.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dahl and Crissman report a June 21, 1947, explosion of a flying saucer over Maury Island on or after June 26, 1947. - HistoryL...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 1947</span></span></span>

Dahl claimed that he went ashore, gathered fragments and attempted unsuccessfully to use his radio. He later showed the material to Crisman, who was variously described as his employer or superior. Crisman then said that he visited the location himself, collected more debris and saw an unusual object over the area. The story also acquired several elements that would become familiar in later UFO folklore:

### A suspicious reporting timeline

The claimed date, 21 June, would place the encounter three days before Kenneth Arnold’s famous sighting near Mount Rainier. However, Washington historian Walt Crowley found that Dahl and Crisman appear to have circulated their account only on or after 26 June, when Arnold’s story had entered national news. Crisman then contacted Raymond Palmer, a publisher associated with science fiction and unusual-phenomena magazines, offering fragments said to have come from the object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylink.org/File/2068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylink.org">[HistoryLink]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylink.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dahl and Crissman report a June 21, 1947, explosion of a flying saucer over Maury Island on or after June 26, 1947. - HistoryL...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 1947</span></span></span>

That timing does not prove fabrication by itself. People sometimes delay reporting strange experiences, especially when they fear ridicule. In this case, however, it matters because the Maury Island account emerged only after “[flying saucers]({{ 'flying-saucers/' | relative_url }})” had become a valuable and widely reported story. Its supposed earlier date allowed it to be presented later as a precursor to Arnold, although there is no secure contemporary record showing that the claim existed before Arnold’s publicity.

The occupational description was also misleading. Dahl and Crisman were frequently called harbour patrolmen, giving their testimony an official appearance. The Air Force account later consulted by Project Blue Book director Edward J. Ruppelt stated that they were not public harbour-patrol officers but operated old boats used to recover floating timber. This does not make them incapable of observing something unusual, but it removes an implied layer of institutional credibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jasoncolavito.com/report-on-ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jasoncolavito.com">[JASON COLAVITO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jasoncolavito.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASON COLAVITOReport on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JASON COLAVITO...</span></span></span>

## How Kenneth Arnold became involved

Ray Palmer paid Kenneth Arnold to travel to Tacoma and investigate the story. Arnold was an obvious choice: his Mount Rainier report had helped launch the national flying-saucer wave only weeks earlier. Arnold was joined by United Airlines pilot E. J. Smith, another reported disc witness. Their involvement gave the Maury Island claim credibility and public attention that Dahl and Crisman could not have generated alone.

In Tacoma, Arnold and Smith examined material supplied as possible wreckage. Accounts describe pale metal fragments and heavier rock-like pieces, but the samples did not convincingly match Dahl’s spectacular description. The thin material appeared unremarkable, while the dark fragments resembled slag — the glassy waste left by industrial smelting — rather than manufactured components from an unknown aircraft. No piece displayed exceptional strength, unusual composition, complex engineering or any other property requiring an exotic explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maury_Island_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maury Island incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maury Island incident</span></span></span>

Arnold was also disturbed by anonymous telephone calls to local newspapers. The callers reportedly knew details of conversations inside his hotel room, leading to suspicions that it had been bugged. The later Air Force account offered a simpler explanation: one of the men associated with the claim was himself supplying information to the press. In other words, the apparent surveillance may have been another device for increasing mystery and publicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jasoncolavito.com/report-on-ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jasoncolavito.com">[JASON COLAVITO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jasoncolavito.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASON COLAVITOReport on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JASON COLAVITO...</span></span></span>

Arnold contacted Lieutenant Frank M. Brown of Fourth Air Force intelligence at Hamilton Field, California. Brown and Captain William L. Davidson flew to Washington, interviewed the participants and inspected the fragments. Their visit turned what had begun as a magazine-sponsored investigation into a matter involving military intelligence.

## The fatal B-25 crash

Brown and Davidson departed McChord Field shortly after 2 a.m. on 1 August 1947 in a TB-25J Mitchell, serial number 44-31316, bound for Hamilton Field. Two other servicemen, Technical Sergeant Woodrow Matthews and Sergeant Elmer Taft, were aboard. Around 18 minutes after take-off, the left engine caught fire. Matthews and Taft escaped by parachute, but the burning wing failed and separated before Brown and Davidson could leave the aircraft. Both officers died when the bomber crashed roughly 15 miles east of Kelso.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://aviation-safety.net/wikibase/83586" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aviation-safety.net">[Aviation Safety Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aviation-safety.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aviation Safety NetworkAccident North American TB-25J Mitchell 44-31316, Friday 1 August 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1947</span></span></span>

The timing was extraordinary: two intelligence officers had investigated an alleged flying-saucer incident and then died on their return journey. Newspaper coverage added reports that the aircraft might have been sabotaged or shot down and that it carried classified material. These statements created the enduring impression that Brown and Davidson had been killed because they were transporting Maury Island debris.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylink.org/File/2068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylink.org">[HistoryLink]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylink.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dahl and Crissman report a June 21, 1947, explosion of a flying saucer over Maury Island on or after June 26, 1947. - HistoryL...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 1947</span></span></span>

The evidence does not support that interpretation. The two survivors described an engine fire and escaped before structural failure destroyed the aircraft. The later Air Force account stated that the engine burned, the wing and tail failed, and the pilots were trapped by the aircraft’s rapid break-up. It also said that the classified cargo consisted of military files the officers had agreed to carry, not secret extraterrestrial wreckage. The accident was real and tragic, but its known sequence is consistent with a catastrophic mechanical fire rather than an attack or deliberate sabotage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://aviation-safety.net/wikibase/83586" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aviation-safety.net">[Aviation Safety Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aviation-safety.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aviation Safety NetworkAccident North American TB-25J Mitchell 44-31316, Friday 1 August 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1947</span></span></span>

Whether the officers carried any of the Maury Island fragments has been described differently in later retellings. Even had samples been aboard, their presence would not establish a connection between the alleged debris and the crash. The decisive point is that investigators had already regarded the material as ordinary and had reportedly expressed strong doubts about the story before leaving McChord.

Brown and Davidson therefore deserve to be remembered as servicemen killed in an aviation accident, not reduced to supporting characters in an unproven conspiracy. Washington’s Senate formally honoured their service in a 2017 resolution marking the incident’s seventieth anniversary. The resolution also repeated a more sympathetic interpretation of Dahl’s later statements, illustrating how the historical record continues to be contested in commemorative and popular accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2017-18/Htm/Bills/Senate%20Resolutions/8648-Maury%20Island%20Incident.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov">[LawFiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Law Files</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Law Files</span></span></span>

## Why investigators called it a hoax

The hoax assessment rests on a combination of failed evidence, false presentation and reported admissions rather than on a single debunking discovery.

**The physical material was ordinary.** The dark fragments were identified in official and later accounts as slag or common rock. No securely documented specimen produced unusual scientific results. A later preliminary examination of material recovered from the [B-25 crash]({{ 'b-25-crash/' | relative_url }}) area found ordinary igneous matter or possibly meteor fragments, neither of which verified Dahl’s story. Material collected from a crash site years later also cannot reliably be assumed to be the same material allegedly examined in Tacoma.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylink.org/File/2068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylink.org">[HistoryLink]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylink.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dahl and Crissman report a June 21, 1947, explosion of a flying saucer over Maury Island on or after June 26, 1947. - HistoryL...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 1947</span></span></span>

**The photographs could not be produced.** Dahl’s supposed images would have been the clearest direct evidence for six enormous objects at close range. Instead, they were said to have been lost or misplaced. Claims that radiation or another force fogged them cannot be evaluated without the negatives, camera or processing records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jasoncolavito.com/report-on-ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jasoncolavito.com">[JASON COLAVITO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jasoncolavito.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASON COLAVITOReport on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JASON COLAVITO...</span></span></span>

**Independent corroboration was missing.** A daylight event involving six very large objects, falling debris, an injured person and a damaged boat should have left multiple traces. Investigators did not obtain persuasive testimony from the other alleged boat occupants, medical documentation, photographs of damage or a securely established debris trail. The more dramatic details depended overwhelmingly on Dahl and Crisman.

**The threatening stranger was not verified.** Ruppelt’s account of the official file stated that the mysterious visitor did not exist. The “man in black” episode nevertheless became one of the case’s most influential features because it supplied a model for later stories about secretive officials silencing UFO witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jasoncolavito.com/report-on-ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jasoncolavito.com">[JASON COLAVITO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jasoncolavito.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASON COLAVITOReport on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JASON COLAVITO...</span></span></span>

The same account said the claim was shaped partly because a publisher wanted a flying-saucer story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jasoncolavito.com/report-on-ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jasoncolavito.com">[JASON COLAVITO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jasoncolavito.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASON COLAVITOReport on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JASON COLAVITO...</span></span></span>

## Did Dahl really confess?

Later accounts sometimes argue that the confession question is less straightforward. Dahl reportedly said at one stage that he would call the affair a hoax to escape publicity and further trouble. Washington’s 2017 Senate resolution adopted the interpretation that his public recantation was itself false and that FBI agents believed he had not genuinely abandoned his original claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2017-18/Htm/Bills/Senate%20Resolutions/8648-Maury%20Island%20Incident.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov">[LawFiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Law Files</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Law Files</span></span></span>

That resolution is evidence of how the incident has been remembered, but it is not an independent reinvestigation of the sighting. Its wording also sits uneasily beside Ruppelt’s account of the official file, which says both men admitted that the fragments were unrelated to flying saucers, and beside the broader record of missing photographs, ordinary debris and invented or misleading details. The exact wording and motivation behind every statement Dahl made may remain debatable; the evidential weakness of the original encounter does not.

A recantation can be unreliable, particularly if made under pressure. Equally, withdrawing a confession does not restore missing photographs, prove that the threatening stranger existed or transform slag into aircraft wreckage. The hoax conclusion is therefore not dependent on accepting one sentence attributed to Dahl. It follows from the cumulative failure of the claim’s testable parts.

## Why no one was prosecuted

The absence of a prosecution has often been presented as evidence that authorities were hiding something. Ruppelt gave a more ordinary explanation. Officials considered legal action but decided that the incident had begun as a foolish joke that expanded beyond its creators’ control. They did not believe Dahl and Crisman could fairly be held responsible for the B-25’s mechanical failure and the deaths of Brown and Davidson.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jasoncolavito.com/report-on-ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jasoncolavito.com">[JASON COLAVITO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jasoncolavito.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASON COLAVITOReport on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JASON COLAVITO...</span></span></span>

There were also practical problems. A prosecution would have required proof of a specific offence, intent and measurable damage directly caused by the false account. It might have brought further publicity to the case while publicly embarrassing Arnold, Smith and military personnel who had taken the report seriously enough to investigate. According to Ruppelt, Arnold and Smith were not immediately told the full hoax assessment partly because officials did not want to humiliate them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jasoncolavito.com/report-on-ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jasoncolavito.com">[JASON COLAVITO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jasoncolavito.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">JASON COLAVITOReport on UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JASON COLAVITO...</span></span></span>

Government silence therefore helped the legend. When the authorities neither prosecuted the claimants nor promptly released a clear public account, later writers could treat that gap as evidence of suppression. The case demonstrates how secrecy surrounding routine military files can create a conspiracy narrative even when the underlying investigation points towards fraud.

## How the story became Washington folklore

Maury Island mattered less as a sighting than as a template. It combined nearly every element that later UFO culture would repeatedly use: recovered wreckage, damaged witnesses, electromagnetic interference, ruined photographs, government investigators, anonymous warnings, classified documents and the sudden deaths of officials. The story was especially powerful because one component — the fatal crash — was indisputably real.

The 1952 book *The Coming of the Saucers*, published by Kenneth Arnold and Raymond Palmer, gave the affair a durable narrative after contemporary public interest had faded. Later authors linked it to the developing “men in black” mythology and to broader claims of official UFO concealment. Each retelling could preserve the striking elements while giving less attention to the reporting delay, ordinary debris and investigators’ hoax findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylink.org/File/2068" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylink.org">[HistoryLink]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylink.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dahl and Crissman report a June 21, 1947, explosion of a flying saucer over Maury Island on or after June 26, 1947. - HistoryL...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 21, 1947</span></span></span>

In Washington, the incident now survives through films, museum events, murals, festivals and state recognition. These commemorations need not imply that the alleged craft were real. They reflect the case’s genuine importance to regional culture and to the history of American UFO belief. The Washington Senate’s anniversary resolution explicitly treated the affair as part of the state’s cultural heritage and recognised its role in popularising the first alleged “man in black”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2017-18/Htm/Bills/Senate%20Resolutions/8648-Maury%20Island%20Incident.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov">[LawFiles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Law Files</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Law Files</span></span></span>

## Was the Maury Island UFO story a hoax?

On the available evidence, the most defensible answer is yes. The central narrative is weakly sourced, apparently reported only after Kenneth Arnold’s sighting became news, and unsupported by its promised photographs or reliable independent witnesses. The alleged wreckage showed no extraordinary properties. Several atmospheric details of mystery were later attributed to the claimants themselves, while official accounts reported admissions that the debris had no connection to a flying saucer.

The B-25 crash does not strengthen the UFO claim. It is well documented as an in-flight engine fire followed by structural failure, with two surviving crewmen who parachuted from the burning aircraft. Claims of sabotage or a secret extraterrestrial cargo arose from early reporting and later speculation, not from demonstrated evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://aviation-safety.net/wikibase/83586" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aviation-safety.net">[Aviation Safety Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aviation-safety.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aviation Safety NetworkAccident North American TB-25J Mitchell 44-31316, Friday 1 August 1947...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1947</span></span></span>

Some uncertainty remains over exactly when Dahl first invented the story, whether he regarded it as a joke, a commercial opportunity or something that had grown beyond his control, and how consistently he admitted deception. Those uncertainties concern the mechanics of the alleged hoax, not strong evidence for six unknown craft over Puget Sound.

Maury Island remains important within Washington’s UFO history because it shows how rapidly a thin claim can become a landmark legend when it intersects with credible investigators, military secrecy, press competition and genuine tragedy. It is best understood not as a confirmed crash-retrieval case, but as an early lesson in separating an extraordinary narrative from the evidence actually left behind.
