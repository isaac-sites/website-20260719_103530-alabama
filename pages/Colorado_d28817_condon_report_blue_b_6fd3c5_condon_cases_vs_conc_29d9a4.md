## Introduction

Did the University of [Colorado]({{ 'what-colorados-ufo-history-really/' | relative_url }})'s UFO study actually support Edward Condon’s famous conclusion that further large-scale UFO research was unlikely to advance science? More than fifty years later, that remains the central dispute surrounding the [Condon Report]({{ 'condon-report/' | relative_url }}). The controversy is not that the study found clear evidence of extraterrestrial craft—it did not. The argument is whether the report’s own case investigations justified such a sweeping recommendation against continued federal study. Several cases remained unexplained, some investigators involved in the project expressed reservations, and critics argued that the report’s summary sounded more definitive than the evidence beneath it. ## What the unresolved cases actually showed

One common misconception is that the Colorado study explained every case it examined. It did not.

The report contained numerous investigations of sightings, photographs, radar incidents and other reports. Some were resolved through conventional explanations such as aircraft, astronomical objects, atmospheric effects or misinterpretation. Others remained unresolved because the available evidence was incomplete or because investigators could not identify a convincing explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condon_Committee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Committee</span></span></span>

The most frequently cited example is the 1956 Lakenheath–Bentwaters radar and visual incident in England. The report's own analysis stated that ordinary explanations could not be ruled out but judged them relatively unlikely, concluding that the probability that at least one genuinely unidentified object was involved appeared “fairly high”. Critics later pointed to this passage as evidence that the report contained stronger mysteries than its public reputation suggested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condon_Committee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Committee</span></span></span>

Other investigators associated with the project also acknowledged a residual category of puzzling reports. Committee consultant Gerald Rothberg later wrote that a small number of cases remained difficult to explain even after detailed examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condon_Committee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Committee</span></span></span>

These unresolved incidents became the foundation of later criticism. Opponents of Condon’s recommendation argued that if trained investigators could not explain some of the best-documented cases, the logical response should have been more research rather than less.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kirkmcd.princeton.edu/JEMcDonald/mcdonald_resa_021269.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kirkmcd.princeton.edu">[Kirk McD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kirkmcd.princeton.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kirk McDUFOs and the Condon Report - A Scientist&#x27;s Critiqueby JE McDonald · Cited by 2 — The Report analyses only about ninety cases, a t...</span></span></span>

## Why unexplained did not mean extraterrestrial

Supporters of the report drew a different conclusion from exactly the same evidence.

The Colorado team's central argument was not that every sighting had been solved. Rather, it was that decades of investigation had failed to produce reliable scientific evidence of a new physical phenomenon. An unexplained case might simply reflect missing data, imperfect observations or limitations in the investigation itself. It did not automatically indicate advanced technology, extraterrestrial visitors or an unknown force of nature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-i.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Section I: Conclusions &amp; RecommendationsAlthough we conclude after nearly two years of intensive study, that we do not see...</span></span></span>

Condon therefore focused on the practical value of future research. His conclusion stated that twenty-one years of UFO investigations had not added to scientific knowledge and that further extensive study was unlikely to be productive. At the same time, he did not argue that all future work should be prohibited. The report explicitly stated that scientists with specific, well-defined proposals should still receive support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-i.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Section I: Conclusions &amp; RecommendationsAlthough we conclude after nearly two years of intensive study, that we do not see...</span></span></span>

This distinction is often overlooked. The report rejected the idea of a permanent, large-scale federal UFO programme. It did not claim that every unidentified observation had been explained, nor did it forbid future scientific investigation of unusual aerial phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-i.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Section I: Conclusions &amp; RecommendationsAlthough we conclude after nearly two years of intensive study, that we do not see...</span></span></span>

## The main criticism of Condon's recommendation

The strongest criticism was not that the report contained errors in every case study. It was that the summary and recommendation appeared broader than the evidence justified.

Astronomer J. Allen Hynek, formerly a consultant to Project Blue Book, argued that the report's introductory conclusions downplayed the fact that a substantial fraction of the investigated cases remained unexplained. He believed the report presented a more dismissive picture than its detailed chapters warranted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condon_Committee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Committee</span></span></span>

Physicist James E. McDonald went further. He argued that the study examined only a small sample of the most significant UFO reports and failed to engage adequately with some of the strongest cases available. In his view, the report's recommendation against continued research was therefore premature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kirkmcd.princeton.edu/JEMcDonald/mcdonald_resa_021269.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kirkmcd.princeton.edu">[Kirk McD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kirkmcd.princeton.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kirk McDUFOs and the Condon Report - A Scientist&#x27;s Critiqueby JE McDonald · Cited by 2 — The Report analyses only about ninety cases, a t...</span></span></span>

Critics also pointed to the famous “Low memo” controversy. Before the project was fully underway, project administrator Robert Low wrote a memorandum suggesting that the study could be conducted by sceptical investigators while appearing objective to the public. When the memo became public, opponents argued that it indicated bias from the beginning. Defenders of the project responded that the memo did not represent Condon’s views and that it did not determine the actual findings of the study.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condon_Committee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Committee</span></span></span>

Some scientists sympathetic to UFO research framed the issue as a logical problem. If a selected group of cases still contained unexplained events after careful analysis, they argued, that residual mystery itself justified further investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condon_Committee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Committee</span></span></span>

## Why many scientists still accepted the conclusion

Despite these criticisms, the report's overall recommendation received important institutional support.

Before publication, the National Academy of Sciences conducted an independent review of the Colorado study. Its review panel agreed that the evidence available from two decades of UFO investigations did not justify assigning a high scientific priority to the subject. For many scientists, the key issue was not whether a handful of reports remained unexplained. Most scientific fields contain anomalous observations. The National Academy panel concluded that the available data did not meet that threshold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condon_Committee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Committee</span></span></span>
