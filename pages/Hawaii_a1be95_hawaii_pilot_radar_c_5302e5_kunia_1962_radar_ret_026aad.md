## Introduction

The April 1962 Kunia radar case is one of the more frequently cited Hawaiian radar mysteries, but it is also one of the weakest documented. The surviving Project Blue Book record describes a ground-radar contact near Oahu that appeared to be moving at roughly 700 knots below 25,000 feet. Yet the same file contains so little technical information that investigators could not determine whether the target represented an aircraft, a radar anomaly, or something genuinely unexplained. Rather than being a strong radar-UFO case, the Kunia incident is best understood as a lesson in how missing evidence can prevent a meaningful conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

## What the Kunia radar file records

The surviving Project Blue Book record card dates the incident to 12 April 1962 and places it near Kunia, Oahu. According to the summary, a ground-radar operator detected an object below 25,000 feet. The return reportedly appeared at approximately 318 degrees on the radar display and showed what was described as a high rate of closure. An estimated speed of about 700 knots was entered into the file. The Air Force evaluation noted that the return could possibly have been an aircraft, but the available information was too limited for a firm identification. The case was left unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

The most important detail is not the reported speed but the sparseness of the documentation. The record card consists largely of a short summary rather than a full technical analysis. No detailed radar plots survive in the public file, and the case lacks the extensive witness statements found in some better-known pilot encounters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

Several critical pieces of information are absent:

## Why the 700-knot estimate is uncertain

Many retellings focus on the reported speed of roughly 700 knots, treating it as evidence of extraordinary performance. The problem is that the surviving file does not explain how that figure was calculated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

Radar-derived speed estimates depend on several variables, including the type of radar, the time interval between observations, the geometry of the target's path and the precision of the recorded positions. Without access to the original track data, it is impossible to verify whether the estimate was based on a sustained track, a brief contact, or a small number of scope observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

A speed of 700 knots was not inherently impossible in 1962. Military aircraft operating during the Cold War routinely exceeded that velocity under some conditions, and radar operators also had to contend with occasional false targets and tracking errors. Without knowing the target's direction of travel, range and duration, the figure cannot be treated as a reliable measure of actual performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

This is one reason the case differs from stronger radar incidents in UFO literature. In better-documented events, investigators can examine multiple radar returns, compare independent sensors, review voice recordings, or correlate radar data with visual sightings. None of that supporting material appears in the surviving Kunia file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

## What evidence is missing?

The Kunia case is often described as unexplained, but that description can be misleading. The Air Force did not conclude that the target displayed extraordinary behaviour. Instead, it effectively concluded that there was not enough information to determine what had happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/2202n-160-40w-kunia-hi-april-1962" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: 2202N 160.40W Kunia, Hawaii, April 1962...April 1, 1962 — On April 12, 1962, near Kunia, Hawaii (about 22-02N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 1962</span></span></span>

Three missing elements are particularly important.

### No preserved radar track

The strongest radar cases usually include plots showing where a target moved and how long it remained visible. In the Kunia case, the publicly available record contains only a brief summary.
