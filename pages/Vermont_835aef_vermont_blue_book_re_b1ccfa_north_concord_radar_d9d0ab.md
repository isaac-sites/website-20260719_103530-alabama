## Introduction

The [North Concord]({{ 'north-concord/' | relative_url }}) radar case remains one of the more intriguing entries in Vermont's Project Blue Book records because it combined two different kinds of evidence that did not fit together neatly. During September 1961, personnel associated with North Concord Air Force Station reported both an unusual [radar target]({{ 'radar-target/' | relative_url }}) and changing coloured lights in the sky. Although investigators eventually suggested that a high-altitude weather balloon was the most likely explanation for the radar contact, the surviving records contain conflicting classifications and leave important questions unanswered. Rather than becoming one of Project Blue Book's officially "unidentified" cases, it occupies an awkward middle ground where the evidence was considered too incomplete for certainty but not strong enough to reject a conventional explanation outright.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/n-concord-afs-vt-september-1961" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">n concord afs vt september 1961</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: N Concord, AFS, Vermont, September 1961 · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: september 1961</span></span></span>

## The radar track and visual reports

North Concord Air Force Station formed part of the Cold War radar network watching North American airspace. On 19 September 1961, operators reported an unusual radar return on a height-finder radar. According to the Project 10073 Record Card, the target initially resembled a normal aircraft-sized return at an altitude of roughly 62,000 feet. Over an observation lasting about 18 minutes, it appeared to move slowly while following an erratic course before radar contact was lost. The Air Force summary specifically noted that its combination of high altitude, relatively low speed and changing direction attracted attention from the operators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/0450-74.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blue Book, 0450-74...</span></span></span>

The surviving [Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book-614925/' | relative_url }}) file also contains a separate visual report describing a round, apparently spinning object seen near Polaris (the North Star). Witnesses said its colour shifted between red, white, blue and green while another dark object was also mentioned. Unlike the radar contact, this visual observation involved changing colours rather than measurable movement, making it difficult to determine whether both reports referred to the same phenomenon or to entirely separate events. The surviving documents do not conclusively link them together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/n-concord-afs-vt-september-1961" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">n concord afs vt september 1961</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: N Concord, AFS, Vermont, September 1961 · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: september 1961</span></span></span>

This distinction is important because radar returns and eyewitness observations carry different strengths and weaknesses. Radar can provide position, altitude and movement, but is vulnerable to atmospheric effects and unusual targets. Visual observations capture appearance but are influenced by distance, lighting and perception. When the two sources cannot clearly be matched, investigators have less confidence in either.

## Why a weather balloon was considered

The Air Force ultimately regarded a weather balloon as the most plausible explanation for the radar target rather than an unknown aircraft or extraordinary object. Several features of the radar track supported that possibility.

Investigators pointed to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the reported altitude of around 62,000 feet, consistent with weather balloons operating in the upper atmosphere;
* the comparatively slow apparent movement;
* an erratic course that could be explained by changing upper-level winds;
* the lack of evidence for rapid acceleration or controlled manoeuvres typical of powered aircraft.

</div>

The comments on the Project 10073 Record Card explicitly state that the combination of low speed, high altitude and irregular movement indicated a "probably high flying weather balloon." The final conclusion on that card was therefore recorded as "Probably Balloon."<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/0450-74.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Blue Book, 0450-74...</span></span></span>

This explanation reflected standard Blue Book investigative practice. High-altitude meteorological balloons were common sources of radar reports during the 1950s and early 1960s because their altitude, reflective surface and wind-driven motion could produce unusual radar tracks.

## How conflicting labels left the case unresolved

Despite the balloon explanation, the paperwork surrounding the case is surprisingly inconsistent.

The GovWeird reconstruction of the National Archives file identifies the case as "Insufficient Data", while also reproducing the original record card showing the conclusion "Probably Balloon". The site additionally notes that the formal USAF evaluation is recorded as "unknown", illustrating that different parts of the surviving documentation do not use the same terminology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/n-concord-afs-vt-september-1961" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">n concord afs vt september 1961</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: N Concord, AFS, Vermont, September 1961 · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: september 1961</span></span></span>

Those mixed labels matter because Project Blue Book distinguished between several different outcomes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Probably Balloon** indicated that investigators believed a balloon best fit the available evidence.
* **Insufficient Data** meant the evidence was inadequate to reach a firm conclusion.
* **Unidentified** was reserved for cases that remained unexplained despite sufficient investigation.

</div>

The North Concord file never settled cleanly into one of those categories. Instead, it preserves signs of an investigation that leaned towards a conventional explanation without fully resolving every element of the report.

Another source of uncertainty is that the radar report and the coloured-light observation were not equally well documented. The radar operators produced measurable information about altitude, duration and direction, while the visual report relied on witness descriptions of colour changes. The surviving file does not demonstrate that investigators established a single cause capable of explaining both observations together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/n-concord-afs-vt-september-1961" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">n concord afs vt september 1961</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: N Concord, AFS, Vermont, September 1961 · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: september 1961</span></span></span>

## Why later researchers still discuss the case

The North Concord incident attracts continued attention because it illustrates the limits of Project Blue Book's investigative process rather than because it provides compelling proof of an extraordinary object.

Researchers generally agree on several points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the radar return was genuinely recorded by military personnel rather than being an invented account;
* the Air Force regarded a weather balloon as the most likely explanation for the radar data;
* the visual observations of changing coloured lights remain more difficult to reconcile with the radar evidence;
* the surviving documentation is too limited to determine with confidence whether both reports described the same object.

</div>

Some later UFO writers have linked the radar incident to the famous Betty and Barney Hill case because both occurred on 19 September 1961 in northern New England. However, the timing and geographical proximity alone do not establish that the events were connected, and historians generally treat them as separate reports unless stronger documentary evidence can demonstrate a direct relationship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/CATEGORIES/09-RADAR_Cases/610919nconcord_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

## What the case shows within Vermont's Blue Book record

Within [Vermont]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-vermonts-ufo/' | relative_url }})'s Project Blue Book archive, the North Concord file stands out not because it conclusively defies explanation, but because it demonstrates how official investigations could produce ambiguous outcomes.
