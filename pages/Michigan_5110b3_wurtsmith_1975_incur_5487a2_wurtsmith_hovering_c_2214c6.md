## How the Original Intrusion Report Described the Object

The earliest reports from Wurtsmith described what security personnel initially believed might be a helicopter. According to accounts derived from military communications, personnel near the rear gate and weapons storage area reported an unidentified aircraft moving at low altitude. The most widely quoted version stated that an “unidentified helicopter” crossed the rear gate and hovered over the weapons storage area before moving away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/751030wurtsmith_rep.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Reportan unidentified helicopter with no lights came up over the back gate and hovered over the weapons storage area at a low al...</span></span></span>

This wording quickly entered official channels. In a high-security Cold War environment, any unidentified aircraft near a Strategic Air Command base was treated as a potentially serious threat. The reference to hovering over the weapons area therefore became the most memorable feature of the incident. Later summaries and UFO literature often repeated the claim as though it were an established fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/751030wurtsmith_rep.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Reportan unidentified helicopter with no lights came up over the back gate and hovered over the weapons storage area at a low al...</span></span></span>

## Why the Later Update Removed the Hovering Claim

The strongest evidence against the hovering story comes from the Air Force's own follow-up message.

After additional information was collected, the description was revised. The updated report still referred to an unidentified low-flying aircraft near sensitive facilities, but it specifically stated that there was “no hovering as previously reported”. The correction did not dismiss the intrusion itself; rather, it narrowed what observers could confidently say about the aircraft's movements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/assort1.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113455-667" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Logistics Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Logistics Agency NMCC. This is an important distinction. The update did not conclude that witnesses had imagined the event, nor did it identify the aircraft. Instead, it withdrew one particular detail—the assertion that the object had remained stationary above the weapons area. The revised version therefore reflects greater uncertainty about the object's precise position and movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/assort1.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113455-667" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Logistics Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Logistics Agency NMCC. Several ordinary factors could explain the change:

* Witnesses were observing at night under stressful conditions.
* Reports were relayed through multiple layers of communication.
* Slow movement toward or away from an observer can sometimes appear stationary.
* Different witnesses may have been describing different moments of the same event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/assort1.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113455-667" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Logistics Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Logistics Agency NMCC. None of these possibilities proves what happened, but they help explain why a dramatic detail might appear in an initial alert and then be removed after review.

## What the Correction Changes About the Case

The correction significantly alters how the Wurtsmith incident should be interpreted.

Without the hovering claim, the case becomes less a story about a craft deliberately inspecting nuclear storage bunkers and more a story about an unidentified aircraft operating near a restricted military installation. That remains noteworthy. Security forces responded, radar operators reported unusual activity, and military aircraft were tasked to investigate. The event was serious enough to generate command-level communications and remain part of the historical record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/assort1.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113455-667" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Logistics Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Logistics Agency NMCC. I,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Logistics AgencyNMCC.
