## Introduction

The unexplained radar targets detected around Washington National Airport during two weekends in July 1952 remain one of the most debated aspects of the wider Chesapeake and Washington sightings. For readers following [Virginia]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-virginias-ufo/' | relative_url }})'s UFO history, the key question is not simply whether radar operators saw "something", but whether the radar was tracking real aircraft or producing false echoes caused by unusual atmospheric conditions. The evidence points to a more complicated picture than either a straightforward aircraft encounter or a complete radar malfunction. Controllers at several facilities reported intermittent unknown targets, yet pilots often failed to find matching objects where the radar indicated they should be. Later technical reviews concluded that the weather was highly favourable for anomalous radar propagation, although some participants remained unconvinced that this explanation accounted for every report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span></span></span>

## What controllers detected on the July weekends

On the nights of 19–20 July and again on 26–27 July 1952, controllers at Washington National Airport observed radar returns that did not resemble ordinary air traffic. Some targets appeared suddenly, travelled at varying apparent speeds, stopped, reversed direction or disappeared before reappearing elsewhere. Reports from Andrews Air Force Base radar operators broadly supported the existence of unusual returns during parts of the incidents, giving the events greater weight than a single unexplained radar display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Washington%2C_D.C._UFO_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1952 Washington, D.C. The radar observations were important because they involved trained personnel working in one of the busiest and most security-sensitive pieces of airspace in the United States. Unlike isolated eyewitness accounts, the operators were accustomed to distinguishing routine aircraft tracks from clutter, weather and equipment artefacts. Their willingness to report the targets therefore attracted immediate military attention.

However, the radar contacts were not continuous or perfectly consistent. Different radar sets did not always display identical targets at the same time, and the returns often appeared only briefly before fading. That inconsistency became central to later attempts to explain the events.

## Why the radar and visual reports did not fully match

One of the strongest arguments against interpreting the radar returns as conventional aircraft is that some visual observations appeared to coincide with radar detections. Yet the overall correspondence between radar and what people actually saw was uneven rather than systematic.

Several patterns emerged:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Fighter aircraft scrambled to investigate frequently failed to acquire matching targets where ground controllers directed them.
* Pilots occasionally reported lights, but these did not always correspond clearly with specific radar returns.
* Ground observers sometimes saw lights in roughly the same direction as radar contacts, but distance, altitude and identity remained uncertain.
* On other occasions, radar showed targets while airborne crews reported nothing unusual at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span></span></span>

</div>

The lack of reliable one-to-one agreement matters because genuinely solid airborne objects would normally be expected to produce more consistent confirmation across multiple observers and instruments. Instead, the evidence resembles a mixture of radar anomalies, occasional visual stimuli and ordinary aircraft operating in [busy airspace]({{ 'busy-airspace/' | relative_url }}).

This does not prove that every radar target was false. Rather, it shows why later investigators regarded the case as much more complicated than popular retellings often suggest.

## How temperature inversions could create false targets

The principal sceptical explanation involves temperature inversions and the associated phenomenon known as anomalous propagation.

Normally, radar beams travel in gently curved paths through the atmosphere. During strong temperature inversions, where warm air lies above cooler air, changes in air density and humidity can bend radar waves more sharply than usual. Under favourable conditions, the beam may strike distant ground objects, buildings or ships before reflecting back to the radar. The display can then show apparent airborne targets that do not correspond to aircraft.

The weather around Washington during both July weekends strongly favoured this effect. Later technical studies examined radiosonde weather-balloon measurements and found super-refractive layers and elevated ducts capable of producing anomalous propagation. The 1968 Condon Report concluded that atmospheric conditions on both weekends were conducive to false radar echoes and judged the unidentified returns to have been "most likely" caused by anomalous propagation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span></span></span>

This explanation also fits several observed features:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* radar targets appeared intermittently rather than continuously;
* apparent movements could result from changing atmospheric conditions rather than object motion;
* different radar installations could be affected differently depending on beam geometry;
* airborne interception attempts frequently failed because no physical aircraft occupied the reported position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span></span></span>

</div>

Importantly, anomalous propagation was already a recognised radar phenomenon in 1952. Controllers knew about it, although unusually strong summer conditions could still create confusing displays.

## Why the explanation remains disputed

Not everyone accepted the inversion explanation. Some radar operators insisted that the returns looked sharper and behaved differently from the clutter they routinely observed. Members of the Air Force investigation also questioned whether atmospheric effects alone could explain every reported track. These reservations have kept the Washington incidents prominent in UFO literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Washington%2C_D.C._UFO_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1952 Washington, D.C. UFO incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1952 Washington, D.C. UFO incident</span></span></span>

At the same time, later technical reviews generally found that the weather evidence strongly supported anomalous propagation. The Civil Aeronautics Administration's own technical assessment reported that temperature inversions were present during almost every period when unexplained radar or visual reports occurred. The Condon study, undertaken independently more than a decade later, reached a broadly similar conclusion after examining the meteorological data in greater detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Washington%2C_D.C._UFO_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1952 Washington, D.C. UFO incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1952 Washington, D.C. UFO incident</span></span></span>

Neither review claimed that every witness was mistaken or that every observation was identical in origin. Instead, they argued that the radar evidence itself did not require unknown craft to explain the recorded events.

## What this means for the Chesapeake and Virginia context

Within the broader story of the 1952 Chesapeake pilot encounters, the Washington radar incidents should not be treated as direct instrumental confirmation of the Nash–Fortenberry sighting near Norfolk. The events occurred on different nights, in different locations and under different circumstances.

Their importance instead lies in showing how a period of heightened attention combined experienced aviation witnesses, sensitive military airspace and imperfect early radar technology. The Washington radar episode illustrates why historians and investigators distinguish between visual sightings and radar evidence rather than assuming they automatically support one another.

The balance of current evidence favours atmospheric propagation as the most plausible explanation for many of the Washington radar targets, particularly given the documented inversion conditions and the repeated failure of interceptor aircraft to find matching objects. Even so, gaps in the surviving records, the absence of preserved radar data and the mixed relationship between radar and visual observations mean that the case remains an instructive example of how ambiguous radar evidence can become part of UFO history without providing conclusive proof either for extraordinary craft or for a single, simple natural explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span></span></span>

    var params = [];
    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
