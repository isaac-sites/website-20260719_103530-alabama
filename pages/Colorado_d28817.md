## Introduction

The evidence is mixed. Colorado has several well-documented reports and genuine episodes of official uncertainty, but no publicly available case establishes extraterrestrial activity. Some incidents remain unresolved because observations were brief or data were incomplete. Others weakened under examination, turning out to involve aircraft, astronomical objects, animal scavenging or unreliable testimony. Colorado therefore offers a useful lesson in what “unidentified” really means: not proven alien, but not yet identified from the evidence available.

## Why Colorado matters in UFO history

Colorado combines several conditions that encourage both sightings and investigation. Large areas have dark skies and long sightlines, particularly across the [San Luis Valley]({{ 'san-luis-valley/' | relative_url }}) and eastern plains. The state also has busy commercial airspace, [military aviation]({{ 'military-skies-15607e/' | relative_url }}), aerospace industries and major defence facilities around Colorado Springs. These create more opportunities to see unfamiliar lights, aircraft, balloons and satellites — but also more observers with aviation or military experience.

The state’s institutional role is even more important. From 1966 to 1968, the US Air Force funded a University of Colorado team led by physicist Edward Condon to evaluate whether UFO reports justified continued scientific study. The project reviewed Air Force records, examined new reports and drew on material from civilian UFO organisations. ## The San Luis Valley: sightings, folklore and “Snippy”

The San Luis Valley is Colorado’s best-known UFO region. It is a broad, high-altitude basin enclosed by mountain ranges, with small settlements, dark nights and views extending for many miles. Reports of unusual lights have accumulated there for decades, although the surviving evidence varies from contemporary documents to recollections collected long after the event.

An early example appears in a declassified account concerning two 19-year-old Pueblo residents who travelled into the valley hoping to photograph unexplained objects. They reported seeing and photographing a strange light. The episode is historically interesting because it shows that the valley already had a reputation for anomalous aerial activity during the Project Blue Book era. It is less useful as proof of anything extraordinary: the available material does not establish the object’s distance, size or physical nature, and the witnesses had entered the area specifically expecting to find UFOs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP81R00560R000100010005-6.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PAIR OF PUEBLO YOUTHS PHOTOGRAPH STRANGE...Quintanilla receives reports from the 100 or so Air Force bases around the country, all al...</span></span></span>

The valley’s reputation became far more famous after the 1967 death of a horse popularly known as [Snippy]({{ 'snippy/' | relative_url }}), although the animal’s registered name was Lady. Its carcass was reportedly found with parts of the head and neck stripped of flesh, while early stories emphasised an apparent absence of blood and unusual marks nearby. Because strange lights had also been reported in the wider region, UFO explanations quickly entered the story.

Later examination substantially weakened the more dramatic claims. Contemporary veterinary and journalistic accounts pointed to natural decomposition and scavenging as plausible explanations for the missing tissue. The supposed lack of blood was not extraordinary for a carcass found after death, and the case produced no verified material showing lasers, radiation or non-human technology. The Denver Public Library’s historical review treats the extraterrestrial interpretation sceptically and highlights how assumption, publicity and repetition transformed an animal-death mystery into UFO folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[history.denverlibrary.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufos and horse called snippy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufos and horse called snippy</span></span></span>

Snippy still matters, but primarily as a case study in how legends develop. A puzzling carcass, reports of lights, an isolated landscape and intense press interest became fused into one narrative even though no evidence directly connected an aerial object with the horse’s death. Later cattle-mutilation stories across the American West repeatedly followed the same pattern.

The valley’s UFO identity has since become part of local culture. The [UFO Watchtower]({{ 'ufo-watchtower/' | relative_url }}) near Hooper, opened in 2000, invites visitors to observe the sky and records reports made at the site. It celebrated its twenty-fifth anniversary in 2025. Such attractions preserve witness stories and show the phenomenon’s cultural importance, but their sighting totals are not equivalent to controlled scientific observations. Reports made by visitors at a UFO-themed destination are affected by expectation, selective attention and the absence of calibrated instruments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cpr.org/2025/04/24/ufo-watchtower-celebrates-25-years/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cpr.org">[Colorado Public Radio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cpr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo watchtower celebrates 25 years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo watchtower celebrates 25 years</span></span></span>

## The Colorado study that helped close Project Blue Book

The University of Colorado UFO Project began amid dissatisfaction with the Air Force’s Project Blue Book. Critics argued that Blue Book was understaffed, too quick to assign conventional explanations and insufficiently scientific. The Air Force hoped an independent university team could provide a more authoritative assessment.

The resulting study examined hundreds of records and presented detailed case studies of 59 reports, including ten older cases considered sufficiently well documented to revisit. It also investigated perception, photography, radar, atmospheric effects and the psychological and social dimensions of UFO reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/nas_re1.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113513-883" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Logistics Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">n detail, with 68 plates; of these, ten reports predated the project, but were so well documented that they...</span></span></span>

Condon’s central conclusion was that further large-scale study was unlikely to produce major scientific discoveries. The report stated that, after more than 10,000 reports accumulated over 19 years, there was no verified case clearly outside known science and technology. It did not claim that every observation had been individually solved. Rather, it argued that the remaining unexplained cases did not amount to evidence of extraordinary craft or justify continuing the Air Force programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-ii.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Section II</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Section II</span></span></span>

The National Academy of Sciences reviewed the project and broadly supported its methods and conclusions. The Air Force subsequently ended Project Blue Book in December 1969. Blue Book had collected 12,618 reports, of which 701 remained classified as unidentified. The Air Force stressed that none showed a national-security threat, technological principles beyond contemporary knowledge or evidence of extraterrestrial vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104590/unidentified-flying-objects-and-air-force-project-blue-book/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span>

### Why the report remains disputed

Critics argued that the report’s conclusion was stronger than its own case material justified. Several investigated reports remained difficult to explain, and some project members believed Condon had formed a dismissive view before the work was complete. Critics also distinguished between two questions that the final conclusions sometimes appeared to merge: whether existing evidence proved extraterrestrial visitation, and whether a small number of unexplained cases deserved further scientific attention.

Those objections have kept the report controversial. Even so, the strongest criticism does not reverse its basic evidential finding. The project found puzzling cases, but no reproducible physical evidence, verified craft or observation demonstrating capabilities beyond known technology. Its lasting significance lies less in “debunking UFOs” than in establishing a demanding question that still applies: what useful conclusion can be drawn when a report lacks reliable information about range, speed, weather, sensor behaviour or alternative traffic?

## Military and aviation connections

Colorado’s defence infrastructure has encouraged speculation about links between UFOs and military activity. Colorado Springs is associated with the North American Aerospace Defense Command, or NORAD, while the wider region contains Space Force and Air Force installations. These organisations monitor aircraft, missiles and objects in space, making them relevant whenever witnesses assume that an unknown light must have appeared on military sensors.

That assumption is often unjustified. A visual observation does not necessarily create a radar return, particularly when the object is small, distant, slow-moving or outside a sensor’s operational settings. Nor does proximity to a defence installation show that an incident involved secret technology. Military areas attract conventional aircraft, helicopters, balloons, satellites and training activity that civilians may not recognise.

Project Blue Book files remain useful because they show what the Air Force actually collected: witness statements, correspondence, newspaper reports, photographs and assessments of possible physical evidence. The National Archives preserves these records, including individual sighting files and related imagery. An “unidentified” classification usually means investigators could not reach a confident conclusion from the surviving information, not that they confirmed an extraordinary vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

A recently publicised military report illustrates that distinction. In 2022, personnel in Colorado Springs observed a shimmering, irregular object described as resembling an angular potato. An initial assessment suggested sunlight reflected from snow-covered mountains may have illuminated the underside of low clouds. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, or AARO, regarded that explanation as low confidence and left the incident unresolved. The released account therefore documents a genuine uncertainty, but not unusual measured performance or evidence of an alien craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jun/12/ufo-uap-files-us-government-release" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Critics and skeptics argue the materials lack credible analysis and context, which may encourage conspiracy theories. This is a recurring feature of military cases. Witness credibility can establish that a person sincerely saw something unusual, but professional status cannot by itself establish distance, size or cause. Pilots and service personnel are trained observers within their specialist tasks, yet unfamiliar viewing angles, atmospheric effects and ambiguous lights can still produce mistaken interpretations.

## The eastern Colorado drone flap

From December 2019 into January 2020, residents and police officers across north-eastern Colorado reported groups of apparent drones flying after dark. Witnesses described repeated routes, grid-like patterns and objects that seemed larger or more numerous than ordinary hobby drones. Similar reports spread into Nebraska and Kansas.

The episode deserves a place in Colorado’s UFO history even though witnesses generally called the objects drones rather than extraterrestrial craft. Like earlier UFO flaps, it involved repeated night-time observations, growing media attention, official uncertainty and increasing numbers of people searching the sky. The FBI, Federal Aviation Administration, Air Force and state authorities made enquiries, while officials contacted airports, drone operators, test facilities, military organisations and NORAD.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/mysterious-drone-swarms-3-states-fbi-investigation-latest-updates-today-2020-01-06/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cbsnews.com">[cbsnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cbsnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CBS News FBI investigating drone swarms startling residents in three</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CBS News FBI investigating drone swarms startling residents in three</span></span></span>

No operator of a large coordinated fleet was publicly identified. However, investigators also failed to substantiate the more dramatic picture of enormous drones systematically sweeping the plains. Colorado officials reported no confirmed criminal incidents or illegal drone operations. Released records indicated that at least some observations were conventional aircraft, stars or planets, while the evidence was insufficient to determine what every witness had seen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcnews.com/US/mystery-drone-faithful-undeterred-lack-evidence/story?id=68264339" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcnews.com">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News&#x27;Mystery drone&#x27; faithful undeterred by lack of evidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC News&#x27;Mystery drone&#x27; faithful undeterred by lack of evidence</span></span></span>

## What the sighting totals do — and do not — show

The National UFO Reporting Center, or NUFORC, lists thousands of Colorado reports and remains one of the most accessible collections of civilian witness testimony. Its state index contained about 3,590 Colorado entries in mid-2026. The reports range from brief descriptions of lights to longer accounts with photographs or multiple witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/subndx/?id=lCO" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nuforc.org.</span></span></span>

These records are valuable for finding cases, comparing recurring descriptions and studying how people report unusual experiences. They should not be treated as a verified count of anomalous craft. NUFORC is a civilian, self-reporting system. Entries may be submitted long after an event, duplicate other observations, contain inaccurate dates or lack the information needed to test ordinary explanations. Researchers using public UAP-reporting data consequently have to account for population, access to dark skies, reporting awareness and proximity to airports or military areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10721628/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Raw totals also exaggerate the importance of densely populated places. Denver and Colorado Springs naturally generate more reports than isolated counties because more people are looking. Conversely, a remote area may acquire a “hotspot” reputation from a comparatively small number of memorable stories.

The most useful Colorado cases are therefore not necessarily those with the strangest descriptions. Stronger reports normally include several independent witnesses, precise times and locations, original photographs or sensor records, weather and astronomical data, and documentation produced before publicity could reshape memories. Very few reports meet all those conditions.

## Why ordinary objects can look extraordinary in Colorado

Colorado’s landscape and technology create recurring sources of mistaken identification.

**Aircraft and helicopters:** Lights approaching almost directly towards a witness can appear stationary before turning suddenly. Mountain terrain may conceal the body of an aircraft while leaving its lights visible.

**Stars and planets:** Bright astronomical objects near the horizon can appear to move because of atmospheric turbulence or the observer’s own motion. Thin clouds can make them change colour or brightness.

**Satellites:** Trains of recently launched Starlink satellites have generated reports around the world. Reflections from individual satellites can also appear, disappear or brighten in ways unfamiliar to observers and pilots. Research reconstructing pilot reports has shown that satellite positions and viewing geometry can reproduce apparently anomalous lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08155" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

**Balloons and drones:** Both may drift silently and lack an obvious scale reference. A small nearby object can be mistaken for something much larger and more distant.

**Atmospheric illumination:** Searchlights, city lighting, ice crystals and sunlight reflected beneath clouds can create apparently structured or moving forms. The tentative explanation offered for the 2022 Colorado Springs report belongs to this category.

These explanations do not prove that every Colorado report is mistaken. They show why eyewitness descriptions alone are rarely enough. Without a dependable distance, even estimates of speed and size can be wrong by orders of magnitude.

## Which Colorado cases remain genuinely unresolved?

Several Colorado reports can reasonably be called unresolved, including parts of the San Luis Valley record, individual Project Blue Book files, some observations during the drone flap and the 2022 Colorado Springs military sighting. In each instance, however, “unresolved” describes the limits of the record.

A useful three-part distinction is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Unresolved:** enough information exists to show that something was observed, but not enough to identify it confidently.
* **Weakly sourced:** the story depends on later retellings, a single uncorroborated witness or missing original records.
* **Plausibly explained or debunked:** ordinary causes fit the evidence better than the extraordinary claim, as with the alien interpretation of Snippy’s death.

</div>

Modern federal reviews reach broadly the same position as the earlier Colorado study. AARO states that it has found no verifiable evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity or technology. Many reports remain open because they lack sufficient scientific data, while resolved cases commonly involve balloons, birds, drones, satellites and aircraft. Remaining unexplained is therefore not itself evidence of advanced performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defense.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/3965403/dod-examining-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dod examining unidentified anomalous phenomena</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod examining unidentified anomalous phenomena</span></span></span>

## Colorado’s lasting UFO legacy

Colorado’s greatest contribution to UFO history is not a crashed saucer or a single classic encounter. It is the contrast between compelling human testimony and the difficulty of turning testimony into reliable physical knowledge.

The San Luis Valley demonstrates how geography and folklore can reinforce one another. Snippy shows how separate mysteries can become joined into an alien narrative. The eastern-plains drone episode shows how a real investigation can end without either identifying every observation or validating the original public picture. Colorado Springs illustrates why even a military report may remain unresolved when the available measurements are poor.

Above all, the Condon Report placed Colorado at the centre of the national argument over whether UFOs should be treated as a scientific problem. Its conclusions were contested, but its central challenge has endured: extraordinary interpretations require more than honest witnesses and unexplained lights. They require evidence capable of separating an unusual object from an unusual view of an ordinary one.
