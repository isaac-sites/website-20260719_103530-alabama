## Introduction

The Congaree radar incident is often cited as one of [South Carolina]({{ 'south-carolina/' | relative_url }})'s most intriguing UFO-related cases because Project [Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book-e3c41a/' | relative_url }}) recorded an apparently extraordinary radar contact in December 1952. However, one of the most important historical questions is not whether the radar target was real, but whether later versions of the story accurately reflect what the Air Force actually documented.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/South_Carolina_11d001_congaree_radar_targe_b2df21_congaree_record_disp_0646dc-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Record Disputes illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/South_Carolina_11d001_congaree_radar_targe_b2df21_congaree_record_disp_0646dc-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/South_Carolina_11d001_congaree_radar_targe_b2df21_congaree_record_disp_0646dc-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

A comparison between the surviving Project Blue Book status report and later UFO catalogues shows that the narrative changed over time. Details that have become familiar in later retellings—including a reported stop, a one-minute hover, a reversal of course and even a different observation time—are absent from the surviving Air Force summary. That does not necessarily prove those later claims are false, but it does mean they are not supported by the principal contemporary record and should be treated as separate, less well-documented traditions rather than part of the original case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AProject_Blue_Book%2C_status_report_8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf - Wikimedia CommonsDecember 31, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1952</span></span></span>

## What Did the Congaree File Actually Report?

The surviving Project Blue Book Status Report No. 8 contains a brief summary rather than a detailed investigative file. Its description is concise and factual.

According to the report:

* the event occurred at **0342 Eastern Standard Time** (3.42 am);
* operators using an **AN/MPS-5 radar** detected an unidentified return approximately 100 miles north-east of Congaree Air Force Base;
* the apparent speed was estimated at roughly **6,000 mph**;
* the contact lasted about **five minutes**;
* there was **no visual observation**, making it a strictly electronic detection;
* the operators were described as experienced and considered reliable;
* investigators nevertheless regarded [radar interference]({{ 'radar-interference/' | relative_url }}) as a serious possibility because the apparent target appeared related to the antenna's rotating sweep.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AProject_Blue_Book%2C_status_report_8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf - Wikimedia CommonsDecember 31, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1952</span></span></span>

Just as important is what the report does **not** contain. It does not describe the target stopping, hovering, reversing direction, making sharp manoeuvres or behaving intelligently. Nor does it provide a detailed sequence of radar plots from which readers could independently reconstruct the reported motion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AProject_Blue_Book%2C_status_report_8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf - Wikimedia CommonsDecember 31, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1952</span></span></span>

## Claims Added by Later UFO Catalogues

As the case circulated through UFO literature during the following decades, some summaries became noticeably more dramatic than the surviving Blue Book record.

Among the additions frequently encountered are claims that the radar target:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* travelled south before stopping;
* remained stationary for roughly one minute;
* reversed along exactly the same track;
* demonstrated deliberate manoeuvring rather than simply high apparent speed.

</div>

Some catalogues also list the observation time as **0842** rather than **0342**. That difference may appear minor, but it is historically significant because the surviving Air Force report clearly records **0342 EST**. Later catalogues generally do not explain how or why the time changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AProject_Blue_Book%2C_status_report_8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf - Wikimedia CommonsDecember 31, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1952</span></span></span>

The stop-and-reversal narrative has become especially influential because it transforms the incident from an unusual radar return into an apparent example of controlled flight. Yet those manoeuvres are not described in the surviving Project Blue Book status report itself. Their documentary origin is therefore much weaker than readers often assume.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AProject_Blue_Book%2C_status_report_8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf - Wikimedia CommonsDecember 31, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1952</span></span></span>

## Why the Differences Matter

Historical UFO research depends heavily on tracing claims back to the earliest surviving documents.

In the Congaree case, the surviving Blue Book summary is a primary source created within the Air Force investigation. Later catalogues are secondary compilations that often condensed, paraphrased or merged information from multiple earlier publications. When the two disagree, historians normally give greater evidential weight to the contemporary official record unless independent documentation supports the later additions.

This distinction matters because the strongest extraordinary claim in the later literature—the reported stop followed by a reversal—is precisely the feature missing from the primary record. Without contemporary documentation, it is impossible to determine whether:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the manoeuvre appeared in a now-lost investigative document;
* it resulted from an error introduced during later compilation;
* or details from another radar case became attached to the Congaree incident during repeated retelling.

</div>

No surviving evidence conclusively resolves that question.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docsteach.org/document/project-blue-book-status-report-number-eight/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docsteach.org">[DocsTeach]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docsteach.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Docs Teach Project Blue Book Status Report Number Eight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book Status Report Number Eight - DocsTeach...</span></span></span>

## How the Source Differences Change the Case

The differences between the original record and later accounts do not eliminate the mystery, but they do alter how strong the case appears.

If one relies only on the surviving Blue Book summary, the evidence supports a modest conclusion: experienced radar operators observed an unusual electronic return that produced an apparently extreme calculated speed, while Air Force investigators simultaneously considered radar interference a plausible explanation.

If, however, one accepts the later additions, the incident becomes considerably more remarkable because an object that stops and reverses course appears much harder to reconcile with simple radar artefacts or calculation errors.

Since those manoeuvres are not preserved in the principal surviving Air Force document, the cautious historical approach is to separate the two versions of the story rather than blend them together. Doing so avoids giving equal evidential status to claims with very different documentary foundations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AProject_Blue_Book%2C_status_report_8.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue Book, status report 8.pdf - Wikimedia CommonsDecember 31, 1952...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 31, 1952</span></span></span>

## A Useful Lesson for South Carolina UFO History

The [Congaree radar case]({{ 'congaree-radar-f3bc98/' | relative_url }}) illustrates a broader pattern found throughout UFO history: stories often accumulate additional detail as they are retold.

That process does not necessarily involve deliberate invention. Information can change through transcription mistakes, abbreviated summaries, repeated quotation, attempts to simplify technical reports or the incorporation of material from parallel sources. Over many decades, those small alterations can substantially reshape how an incident is remembered.

For readers examining South Carolina's UFO history, the Congaree case therefore serves two purposes.         });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
