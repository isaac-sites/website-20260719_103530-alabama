## Introduction

One of the most common reasons that unusual lights are reported from aircraft over [Alaska]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-alaskas-skies/' | relative_url }}) is not that the lights are moving, but that they only appear to be moving. In the state's vast night skies, pilots often fly above dark oceans, mountains, tundra and ice fields with few visual reference points. Under those conditions, a bright star, planet or distant ground light can seem to pace an aircraft, alter course, or even follow it for many minutes.

This effect matters when evaluating Alaska UFO reports because many sightings involve experienced aircrew who sincerely believed they were watching an object manoeuvre alongside them. ## How the Autokinetic Illusion Creates Apparent Motion

The best-known mechanism is the autokinetic illusion, often shortened to autokinesis. The phenomenon occurs when a person stares at a single point of light against a dark, featureless background. Although the light is stationary, it can appear to drift, dart, wobble or slowly change direction. The effect is recognised in aviation training because pilots may mistake a star, planet, beacon or distant aircraft light for an object that is actively manoeuvring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aopa.org/training-and-safety/online-learning/safety-spotlights/spatial-disorientation/tricked-by-illusions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aopa.org">[aopa.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aopa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tricked by IllusionsAutokinesis: At night, a stationary dim light against a dark background will appear to move if a pilot visually f...</span></span></span>

The illusion arises because the eyes are never perfectly still. Tiny involuntary eye movements normally go unnoticed because the brain compares them with surrounding visual references. When almost everything outside the cockpit is dark, those reference points disappear. The brain can then misinterpret the eye movements as motion by the light itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eazypilot.com/blog/night-illusions-while-flying-what-every-pilot-should-know/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eazypilot.com">[eazypilot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eazypilot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">night illusions while flying what every pilot should know</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Autokinesis. Alaska provides especially favourable conditions for this illusion:

* Long winter nights increase the amount of flying conducted in darkness.
* Large regions have little or no ground lighting.
* Snow-covered terrain may blend into darkness, removing surface detail.
* Flights over ocean, ice or remote wilderness often leave crews with few visual cues beyond isolated lights in the distance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/12_afh_ch11.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airplane Flying Handbook (3C) Chapter 11In the dark, a stationary light will appear to move about when stared at for many seconds. To the observer, the motion can look entirely real.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hartzellprop.com/watch-out-for-night-flight-illusions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hartzellprop.com">[hartzellprop.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hartzellprop.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">watch out for night flight illusions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">watch out for night flight illusions</span></span></span>

## Why Aircraft Turns Strengthen the Impression of Pursuit

Many Alaska sighting narratives include a striking claim: a light appeared to follow the aircraft through multiple heading changes. At first glance, that sounds difficult to reconcile with a distant star or planet. In reality, aircraft motion can strengthen the illusion.

Very distant objects move differently across a pilot's field of view than nearby objects. A star, planet or far-off ground light may remain in roughly the same position relative to the windscreen even while the aircraft covers many miles. Because the object shows little apparent displacement, it can seem to be matching the aircraft's course.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aopa.org/training-and-safety/online-learning/safety-spotlights/spatial-disorientation/tricked-by-illusions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aopa.org">[AOPA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aopa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tricked by IllusionsAutokinesis: At night, a stationary dim light against a dark background will appear to move if a pilot visually f...</span></span></span>

During a turn, the geometry becomes even more deceptive. The aircraft changes heading while the distant light remains fixed in space. To the crew, the light may slide across the windscreen and then settle into a new position after the turn is completed. That sequence can create the impression that the object deliberately adjusted its course in response to the aircraft's manoeuvre. FAA flight-training materials specifically warn that stationary lights viewed in darkness can appear to move and lead pilots to incorrect conclusions about another object's trajectory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/airplane_handbook/12_afh_ch11.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airplane Flying Handbook (3C) Chapter 11In the dark, a stationary light will appear to move about when stared at for many seconds. The...</span></span></span>

This is one reason investigators are cautious about reports stating that an unknown light "paced" an aircraft. Unless independent radar, multiple viewpoints or precise tracking data exist, apparent pacing alone is not reliable proof that the object was actually manoeuvring.

## How False Horizons and Black-Hole Conditions Mislead Crews

The apparent pursuit effect often becomes more convincing when combined with other night-flying illusions.

A false horizon occurs when pilots unconsciously treat clouds, stars, mountain silhouettes or patterns of lights as the true horizon. In remote areas of Alaska, where the real horizon may be invisible, these substitute references can distort a pilot's perception of both aircraft attitude and external objects. A light that is actually stationary may seem to climb, descend or bank relative to the aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak/19_phak_ch17.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chapter 17: Aeromedical FactorsAutokinesis. When flying in the dark, a stationary light may appear to move if it is stared at for a pr...</span></span></span>

Another relevant phenomenon is the black-hole environment. Aviation literature uses this term for situations in which an aircraft operates over dark, featureless terrain or water with few peripheral visual cues. Under black-hole conditions, judging distance, altitude and relative motion becomes much more difficult. Alaska's extensive coastlines, frozen landscapes and sparsely populated interior frequently create exactly this visual environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-05/FAA-H-8083-3B%20Chapter%2010.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atsb.gov.au">[atsb.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atsb.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on atsb.gov.au.</span></span></span>

When autokinesis, false horizons and black-hole conditions occur together, a crew may experience a powerful but misleading impression that a distant light is actively moving around them.

## Why Bright Planets Are Frequent Candidates

Some of the most convincing "following light" reports involve exceptionally bright celestial objects.

Venus, Jupiter and occasionally bright stars such as Sirius can appear unusually intense when viewed through layers of atmosphere near the horizon. Atmospheric turbulence may cause them to shimmer, flash different colours or seem to change brightness.
