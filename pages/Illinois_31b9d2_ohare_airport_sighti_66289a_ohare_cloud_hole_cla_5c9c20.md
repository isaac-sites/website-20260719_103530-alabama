## Introduction

The most discussed piece of physical evidence in the 2006 Chicago O’Hare sighting is not the object itself but what witnesses said happened after it departed. Several airline employees reported that the dark object shot vertically into a low overcast cloud layer and left behind a sharply defined circular opening through which blue sky could be seen. For supporters of the case, this “cloud hole” is important because it appears to describe a physical effect on the environment rather than a fleeting visual misidentification. For sceptics, it is also the weakest part of the evidence because no verified photograph, video, radar record or meteorological observation confirms that the opening existed. The cloud hole therefore sits at the centre of the debate: it is the most dramatic claim, but also the one most dependent on witness testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5cf80ff422b5a90001351e31/t/5db33f31d35a623422d8010c/1572028245437/TR10_1edition.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static1.squarespace.com">[Squarespace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static1.squarespace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Case 18 Main Text 1 Rev</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">07/24/07 R.F.Haines et al. ## What witnesses said they saw after the object departed

According to accounts gathered by investigators and journalists, the reported object hovered beneath O’Hare’s low cloud ceiling before accelerating upwards. Witnesses then described a circular or near-circular opening in the overcast directly above the departure point. Several said they could see blue sky through the gap. Some accounts compared the effect to a hole being punched through a solid layer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5cf80ff422b5a90001351e31/t/5db33f31d35a623422d8010c/1572028245437/TR10_1edition.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static1.squarespace.com">[Squarespace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static1.squarespace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Case 18 Main Text 1 Rev</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">07/24/07 R.F.Haines et al. The descriptions were broadly similar but not identical. Estimates of the hole’s size varied, as did estimates of how long it remained visible. Some witnesses suggested only a few minutes, while others recalled the opening persisting for ten minutes or more before gradually closing. NARCAP, the National Aviation Reporting Center on Anomalous Phenomena, regarded these reports as significant enough to analyse separately as a “hole-in-cloud” phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5cf80ff422b5a90001351e31/t/5db33f31d35a623422d8010c/1572028245437/TR10_1edition.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static1.squarespace.com">[Squarespace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static1.squarespace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Case 18 Main Text 1 Rev</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">07/24/07 R.F.Haines et al. ## How cloud height, haze and perspective affect the claim

The weather conditions recorded at O’Hare on the afternoon of 7 November 2006 complicate efforts to evaluate the reports. Weather observations indicated an overcast ceiling of roughly 1,900 feet above ground level and visibility reduced by haze. Those conditions created a relatively uniform grey background against which any unusual shape or contrast could stand out strongly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5cf80ff422b5a90001351e31/t/5db33f31d35a623422d8010c/1572028245437/TR10_1edition.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static1.squarespace.com">[Squarespace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static1.squarespace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Case 18 Main Text 1 Rev</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">07/24/07 R.F.Haines et al. However, those same conditions make distance and scale difficult to judge. Observers looking upward at a low cloud deck cannot easily determine whether a dark object is close and small or farther away and larger. The apparent size of a hole in the cloud layer is equally difficult to estimate without reference points. A circular opening viewed at an angle can also appear distorted, while breaks in cloud texture may seem sharper than they actually are when seen against an otherwise featureless overcast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5cf80ff422b5a90001351e31/t/5db33f31d35a623422d8010c/1572028245437/TR10_1edition.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static1.squarespace.com">[Squarespace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static1.squarespace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Case 18 Main Text 1 Rev</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">07/24/07 R.F.Haines et al. Haze adds another complication. Reduced visibility can soften edges and affect depth perception, particularly when observers are looking nearly vertically. In practical terms, witnesses may accurately report seeing an opening without being able to determine its exact dimensions, altitude or cause. This does not mean the opening was imaginary, but it does mean that precise physical conclusions cannot be drawn from the visual descriptions alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5cf80ff422b5a90001351e31/t/5db33f31d35a623422d8010c/1572028245437/TR10_1edition.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static1.squarespace.com">[Squarespace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static1.squarespace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Case 18 Main Text 1 Rev</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">07/24/07 R.F.Haines et al. ## Could a known weather phenomenon explain the opening?

Shortly after the case became public, some meteorologists pointed to a known atmospheric phenomenon called a hole-punch cloud, also known as a fallstreak hole. These are genuine circular openings that can form in cloud layers when supercooled water droplets suddenly freeze and evaporate, often after being disturbed by an aircraft passing through the cloud. The resulting gap can appear surprisingly neat and can reveal blue sky above.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.weather.gov/grb/111506_holepunch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.gov">[weather.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Weather Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 15, 2006 Hole Punch CloudsNov 15, 2006 — Known as &quot;hole punch clouds,&quot; the holes develop as ice crystals above the main cloud la...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 15, 2006</span></span></span>

The existence of hole-punch clouds demonstrates that circular openings in cloud layers do not require an extraordinary explanation. In fact, unusual cloud holes have repeatedly been mistaken for UFO-related phenomena because of their striking appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallstreak_hole" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fallstreak hole</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fallstreak hole</span></span></span>

Yet the comparison is not a complete solution to the O’Hare case. Witnesses were not merely reporting a cloud hole appearing on its own. They described seeing an object beneath the cloud layer first and then observing an opening immediately after the object's rapid ascent. The question therefore becomes whether the sequence was genuinely connected or whether observers linked two separate phenomena occurring at nearly the same time.
