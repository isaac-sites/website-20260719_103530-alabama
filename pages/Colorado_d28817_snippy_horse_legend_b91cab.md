## Introduction

The horse remembered as “Snippy” was found dead near Alamosa in [Colorado]({{ 'what-colorados-ufo-history-really/' | relative_url }})’s [San Luis Valley]({{ 'san-luis-valley/' | relative_url }}) in September 1967. Reports described a startling carcass: flesh apparently stripped from the head and neck, exposed bone, little visible blood and unexplained marks nearby. Newspapers connected the death with local stories of strange lights, and the case became one of America’s earliest and most influential UFO-linked animal-mutilation legends.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Colorado_d28817_snippy_horse_legend_b91cab-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Snippy" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Later investigation did not support the idea that a flying saucer had killed or dissected the horse. Veterinary findings pointed instead towards infection, followed by ordinary decomposition and scavenging. Crucial claims about radiation, landing marks and surgical precision were poorly documented or could not be independently tested. The precise cause of death was never established beyond all doubt, but the surviving evidence weighs much more heavily towards a mundane death than an exotic one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

## What was reported at the carcass

The animal was a three-year-old Appaloosa mare kept at the Harry King Ranch near Alamosa. Although almost universally called Snippy in later accounts, she was reportedly named Lady; “Snippy” appears to have entered the story through confused or inaccurate early reporting. She failed to return to the ranch on 7 September 1967, and her remains were found about a quarter of a mile from the ranch house two days later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

The condition of the carcass was undeniably unusual to those who saw it. Witnesses said the head and neck had been stripped of skin and flesh, leaving pale exposed bone, while much of the rest of the body appeared comparatively untouched. They reported no obvious pool of blood and described the margins of the missing tissue as remarkably clean. These observations became the factual core around which increasingly dramatic claims accumulated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

Other details were less stable. Accounts mentioned a sweet or medicinal smell, bones that changed from white to pink and later darkened, and a burning or itching sensation after contact with material from the scene. Dark patches on the ground were interpreted as scorch or exhaust marks. Witnesses also described flattened vegetation and six small circular depressions nearby, which were later presented as possible landing-gear impressions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

The problem is that most of these observations were not recorded under controlled conditions. The alleged colour changes and skin irritation were temporary. The ground marks were not shown to have been created at the time of the horse’s death, much less by an aircraft. Reports of elevated radiation were inconsistent, and the instruments, background readings and exact testing procedures were not adequately documented. The carcass was also examined after decomposition and animal activity had already begun, making a reliable reconstruction much harder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

Even the claim that there were no tracks needs caution. It was based principally on witness recollection rather than a preserved forensic survey. People had already approached and revisited the carcass, and the absence of obvious predator tracks would not exclude birds, insects or small scavengers. A mysterious-looking scene is not necessarily a scene preserved exactly as it appeared at the moment of death.

## How UFO claims entered the story

No witness reported seeing a UFO attack the horse. The extraterrestrial interpretation arose indirectly, by combining the carcass with unrelated or loosely connected reports of unusual lights in the San Luis Valley. One elderly resident reportedly recalled a large object passing overhead around the time the mare disappeared, but she could not identify it clearly. No observation established that the object landed, approached the horse or had any physical connection with its death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutilation_of_%22Snippy%22_the_horse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span></span></span>

The story nevertheless arrived at an ideal moment for national attention. Flying-saucer reports were already prominent in American culture, while the US Air Force and the University of Colorado were conducting a major study of UFO claims. Headlines framed the incident as a possible encounter with a flying saucer before [veterinary evidence]({{ 'veterinary-evidence/' | relative_url }}) had been properly developed. Once that framing was established, ordinary uncertainties — missing blood, pale bone, marks in the soil — were interpreted as signs of extraordinary technology rather than questions requiring separate explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newspapers.com/article/55072223/snippy_mutilation_1967/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newspapers.com">[newspapers.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newspapers.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">snippy mutilation 1967</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Snippy Had To Be, Says Colo. This process also changed the case as it travelled. Details varied between newspaper reports and later retellings. Claims about missing organs, perfect cauterisation, strong radiation and precisely arranged landing marks became more definite in some versions than the contemporary evidence justified. Even the animal’s name and sex were repeatedly confused. Such instability does not prove that every unusual observation was false, but it shows how quickly reporting and folklore became entangled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutilation_of_%22Snippy%22_the_horse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span></span></span>

Civilian UFO investigators were not unanimous in supporting the saucer theory. By December 1967, an investigator associated with the National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena reportedly judged that the death was neither meaningfully a UFO case nor especially mysterious. That early scepticism is important: rejection of the alien explanation was not simply imposed decades later by people unfamiliar with the period’s evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutilation_of_%22Snippy%22_the_horse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span></span></span>

The University of Colorado’s Air Force-funded UFO project also examined the incident. Its case assessment found no evidence that the horse’s death was associated with abnormal causes. This conclusion did not identify every detail with certainty, but it rejected the central leap from an unusual carcass to an extraordinary aerial intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazette.com/2022/05/22/ufo-legend-horse-found-dead-and-mutilated-55-years-ago-in-colorado-gets-new-life-at-roadside-attraction-130b0e6c-ca4a-11ec-af09-ffeb7d7ab0f4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazette.com">[Gazette]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazette.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO legend: Horse found dead and mutilated 55 years ago...22 May 2022 — The Condon report concluded: “There was no evidence to su...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2022</span></span></span>

## What veterinary examination found

The strongest evidence against an exotic explanation came from examination of the remains. Local veterinarian Wallace Leary later prepared the skeleton for display and reportedly found two small-calibre bullet wounds in the mare’s flank. The wounds showed evidence of infection, providing a plausible route by which the horse could have weakened and died without any connection to the condition later observed around its head and neck.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

Later accounts said two local students admitted shooting the horse, although they maintained that the shooting occurred before the death became famous and had not been intended as part of a hoax. The confession does not by itself establish the exact medical sequence, and the surviving accounts differ on timing and circumstances. It does, however, reinforce the veterinary finding that an overlooked injury and infection were present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutilation_of_%22Snippy%22_the_horse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mutilation of &quot;Snippy&quot; the horse</span></span></span>

This is a major weakness in the original mystery narrative. Early observers treated the carcass’s visible surface condition as if it provided a complete account of the death. Yet the later examination identified injuries that had initially been missed. Once potentially fatal infection is admitted, the case changes from “What removed the flesh and killed the horse?” to two separate questions: what caused death, and what happened to the body afterwards?

Those questions need not have the same answer. An animal can die from disease or injury and then acquire apparently precise damage through post-mortem processes. Scavengers commonly begin with exposed, soft or easily accessible tissues. Birds and insects may remove flesh without leaving the large paw prints or signs of struggle expected from a predator attack. Drying can tighten tissue edges and make irregular damage appear sharper than it was when fresh. Blood also settles internally, coagulates, is consumed by insects or dries rapidly on exposed ground, so its apparent absence does not demonstrate that a carcass was mechanically drained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/92751621/Maggots_mutilations_and_myth_Patterns_of_postmortem_scavenging_of_the_bovine_carcass" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[academia.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">· Claims of human involvement in cattle mutilations require elimination</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Patterns of postmortem scavenging of the bovine carcassJanuary 1, 1989 — Postmortem scavenging of cattle carcasses reveals patter...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 1989</span></span></span>

The dry climate of the San Luis Valley may have made the remains look especially strange. Desiccation can preserve some parts of a carcass while exposed areas are rapidly stripped or dried. Pale bone can later change colour through moisture, remaining tissue, soil, microbial activity or weathering. None of these processes requires a laser, cauterising instrument or unknown chemical.

## Why the carcass looked “surgical”

Descriptions such as “clean”, “bloodless” and “surgical” have become standard language in animal-mutilation stories, but they are interpretations rather than diagnoses. A cut cannot be established as surgical merely because its edge looks smooth in a photograph or after drying. A competent examination would need fresh tissue, microscopic study of the wound margins and evidence that the damage occurred while the animal was alive.

This distinction was often missing from reports about Snippy. By the time experts examined the remains, much of the tissue needed to distinguish cutting from scavenging had disappeared. The original site had not been secured, and no full contemporary post-mortem examination preserved the carcass in its initial state. Consequently, the claim of precise removal could not be verified to modern forensic standards.

Later livestock-mutilation investigations showed how deceptive carcasses can be. New Mexico’s Operation Animal Mutilation examined reports that included bloodless bodies, missing soft tissue and apparently precise incisions. Its investigator concluded that the great majority involved animals that had died from ordinary injuries, poisoning or disease before being altered by scavengers and decomposition. These later cases cannot prove exactly what happened to Lady, but they demonstrate that the features called extraordinary in 1967 can arise naturally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.newyorker.com/news/letter-from-the-southwest/the-enduring-panic-about-cow-mutilations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: newyorker.com">[newyorker.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">newyorker.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New Yorker The Enduring Panic About Cow Mutilations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New Yorker The Enduring Panic About Cow Mutilations</span></span></span>

The natural explanation is therefore cumulative rather than dependent on one perfect reconstruction. There was a plausible source of fatal infection. Scavenging and drying can account for the missing flesh and bloodless appearance. The supposed landing evidence was never tied to an aircraft. The radiation claims were not reproducible. Most importantly, there was no direct observation or physical evidence connecting the horse with a UFO.

## How one death became a national legend

Snippy matters in Colorado’s UFO history less because the case remained scientifically compelling than because it created a durable storytelling pattern. It joined an isolated rural death, local aerial rumours, ambiguous physical traces and dramatic press coverage into a single narrative. Similar elements would recur during the cattle-mutilation scares of the 1970s: mysteriously dead livestock, absent blood, supposedly surgical cuts, unidentified lights and suspicion of hidden operators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/cattle-mutilation-1970s-skinwalker-ranch-ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cattle mutilation 1970s skinwalker ranch ufos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cattle mutilation 1970s skinwalker ranch ufos</span></span></span>

The case also illustrates how a weakly documented claim can become stronger in cultural memory. Details that were uncertain in 1967 were repeated as established facts. The horse’s mistaken name became permanent. Doubts about the carcass became assertions of technological precision, while the failure to identify one definitive cause was treated as support for aliens. Later books, television programmes and tourism kept the most mysterious version alive even after veterinary and official assessments had weakened it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

It passed through several owners, was displayed as a curiosity and was eventually taken to the [UFO Watchtower]({{ 'ufo-watchtower/' | relative_url }}) in the San Luis Valley. ## Did a UFO kill Colorado’s famous horse?

There is no good evidence that it did. The horse’s death was not witnessed, the alleged aerial activity was not directly connected with the animal, and the supposed physical traces were either transient, poorly recorded or open to ordinary explanations. Veterinary examination found infected bullet wounds, while decomposition, insects and scavengers offer a credible explanation for the carcass’s dramatic appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/western-history/ufos-and-horse-called-snippy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.denverlibrary.org">[Denver Public Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.denverlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on denverlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

It remains reasonable to say that the exact sequence cannot now be reconstructed with certainty. The scene was not investigated promptly as a forensic case, and important tissues were lost before specialist examination. That uncertainty, however, should not be confused with equal support for every proposed explanation. Infection and post-mortem animal activity are known processes supported by physical findings. Alien intervention is an inference built from disconnected sightings and ambiguous marks.

Snippy’s real importance is therefore historical. The case shows how an unexplained animal death can become UFO folklore when striking appearances, imperfect investigation and sensational reporting reinforce one another. Within Colorado’s wider UFO story, it is best treated not as evidence of extraterrestrial activity, but as an influential example of how legends form around evidence that is incomplete, unstable and ultimately more ordinary than the headline suggests.
