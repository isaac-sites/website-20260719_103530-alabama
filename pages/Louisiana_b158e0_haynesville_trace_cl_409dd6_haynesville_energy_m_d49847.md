## Introduction

The short answer is that the 2025 [Haynesville]({{ 'haynesville/' | relative_url }}) energy model is technically interesting but only as reliable as the assumptions fed into it. The paper attempted to estimate how much radiant energy would have been required to produce the extraordinary brightness reported by witness Louie Galloway and, if the later bark samples genuinely came from the correct site, how much energy might have been needed to affect vegetation. The resulting figures were extremely large and attracted attention because they appeared to imply a powerful physical event rather than a simple misperception. However, the calculations depend on several uncertain inputs: the distance to the light source, the true location of the event, the accuracy of brightness estimates made decades earlier, and the provenance of the bark samples themselves. As a result, the model can show what energy would be required under a particular set of assumptions, but it cannot demonstrate that those assumptions are correct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. Within the wider debate over Haynesville's alleged physical traces, this distinction is crucial. A sophisticated calculation can quantify a scenario, but it cannot rescue weak or uncertain evidence. The reliability of the model therefore depends less on mathematics than on the quality of the underlying historical data.

## What the 2025 model tried to calculate

The 2025 aerospace paper revisited the Haynesville case nearly six decades after the original sighting. Its goal was not simply to retell the event but to estimate the radiative energy associated with the reported luminous phenomenon. Using witness descriptions, reconstructed geometry and later physical-trace claims, the authors attempted to calculate the energy levels that would have been required to create the observed effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. The basic logic is straightforward. If a light source was bright enough to illuminate a forest, overpower the apparent effect of vehicle headlights and cast shadows through trees in poor weather conditions, then physics can be used to estimate the minimum energy output needed to produce those observations. Likewise, if bark was genuinely altered by exposure to intense radiation, one can estimate the energy necessary to create such changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. In that sense, the model is a legitimate scientific exercise. The question is not whether the calculations themselves are possible, but whether the historical inputs are sufficiently secure to justify confidence in the final numbers.

## Which witness and site assumptions drive the result

Several assumptions dominate the calculation. Small changes in these inputs can produce very large changes in the estimated energy requirement.

### Distance to the source

Distance is perhaps the most important variable. Radiative intensity falls rapidly with increasing distance. If the light was twice as far away as assumed, the required energy output rises dramatically. Yet the Haynesville witness never measured the object's distance directly. Estimates were reconstructed from bearings, road positions and later site investigations rather than from contemporaneous instrumentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. They are...Read more...</span></span></span>

This means the model is not calculating the energy of a known source at a known location. It is calculating the energy of a hypothetical source placed at a reconstructed location.

### Brightness estimates

The witness descriptions are vivid but qualitative. Statements that the light illuminated woodland, cast shadows or appeared brighter than vehicle headlights are valuable observations, yet they are not photometric measurements.

To convert such descriptions into energy values, researchers must translate human impressions into numerical estimates. That process inevitably introduces uncertainty. Weather conditions, dark adaptation, fog, rain and memory effects can all influence perceived brightness. The mathematical treatment may be rigorous, but the observational input remains approximate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. ### The reconstructed site

A further complication is that the location associated with the later bark samples was not documented as a confirmed trace site immediately after the sighting. The original investigations in 1967 did not report finding the dramatic vegetation damage that later became associated with the case.

The 2025 study relied on a reconstructed location identified after the event through witness recollections and later field work. The paper itself acknowledges the importance of site reconstruction because the geometry of the event affects every subsequent calculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. If the selected clearing was not the exact location involved in the sighting, then any energy estimate derived from vegetation at that site becomes correspondingly less secure.

The most striking energy estimates emerge when the model is linked to the reported bark damage. This creates a chain of reasoning:

The crucial point is that energy calculations do not independently verify the bark evidence. They assume the bark evidence is connected to the event and then calculate what would be required if that assumption is true. If the chain breaks at any point, the resulting energy figures lose much of their evidential value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. This is a common misunderstanding in discussions of physical-trace cases. Large numbers can appear impressive, yet they remain conditional upon the authenticity and relevance of the physical traces being modelled.

## Why impressive numbers cannot validate uncertain evidence

One reason the 2025 study attracted attention is that its estimates suggest an energy output far beyond ordinary vehicle lights, household lighting or many conventional explanations. Such figures naturally create the impression that something extraordinary must have occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042125000247" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High-energy events are not rare in the context of UFO/UAP reports. They are...Read more...</span></span></span>

However, there is a logical distinction between calculating the consequences of an assumption and proving the assumption itself.

For example, if one assumes a particular source produced a certain level of illumination, physics may show that the source would need to generate enormous energy. That result demonstrates the implications of the scenario. It does not demonstrate that the scenario actually happened.

The Haynesville model therefore has greater value as a sensitivity analysis than as proof. It shows that, under the study's chosen assumptions, the reported observations imply a very energetic phenomenon.
